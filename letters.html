Letter 003 · 29 August 2026

Marks are opinions, and mine are generous

The book is up 4.62% on invested capital and all 41 positions are marked above cost. I want to start by saying clearly that this is not a track record. It is an artifact of three things: roughly 96% of the cost basis went to work after the first of May, the marks come from public listings rather than from anything I have sold, and I have never sold anything. A portfolio in which nothing has been realized and everything is green is not telling you about skill. It is telling you about the direction of the aggregator feed.

Two specific marks deserve to be challenged, and it is better that I challenge them than that a buyer does it for me later.

The first is The Hobbit, which is 34.8% of net asset value and the largest thematic exposure in the book. I bought a Collector Booster Display on 18 July at 839.85 and a Collector Booster Box on 13 August at 795.00. Wizards priced the Collector Booster at 37.99, which puts a twelve-pack box at 455.88 at retail. So I paid roughly eighty percent over the cost of the cardboard inside, during the window between the end of preview season and the release date, which is historically the single worst window in which to buy a Magic sealed product. Market value was running near 870 in the days before launch. I did not buy the top by accident; I bought a hype price on purpose and told myself it was a Tolkien position.

The precedent is not comforting. Collector Booster boxes for the Spider-Man and Avatar crossovers were bid toward a thousand dollars in presale and then settled back to roughly 750 and 525 once product actually reached shelves. Both remain above the retail cost of the packs, so this is not a catastrophe, but anyone who bought at the presale peak took a punishing round trip. The bull case for The Hobbit is that Final Fantasy did the opposite and recovered strongly. That recovery had a specific mechanism behind it, which was a chase card that stayed genuinely scarce in sealed product for a long stretch. The Hobbit has a candidate for that role in the Gleaming Gold Smaug, printed at roughly five hundred copies and appearing in under one percent of Collector Boosters. Whether that is enough is an open question, and it will not be answered for a year. Until it is, I am treating the Hobbit block as marked at a price set by scarcity of supply on shelves rather than by settled demand, and I am not adding to it.

The second challenged mark is on the vintage side, and it is more embarrassing because vintage is supposed to be the safe half of the book. I hold a sealed Fallen Empires Booster Box acquired on 8 August at 534.99 and carried at 543.92. Recent completed eBay sales for the same product have printed in the 365 to 386 range. That is a wide gap in the wrong direction, and it points at the structural problem with marking from public price data: listed prices and sold prices are different numbers, and on thinly traded vintage sealed the difference can be a third of the value. A handful of optimistic listings can set an aggregator's market price for months. The right response is not to argue with the comp. It is to change the policy.

So, three changes. Valuation moves to a sold-price basis where completed sales exist, with listings used only where no sale is observable and flagged as such in the ledger. The Hobbit block gets reviewed against post-release prices in October rather than carried at a pre-release mark. And a hard rule going forward: no acquisition inside the thirty days before a set's release date. That rule would have prevented both of the purchases discussed above, which is precisely the point of writing it down while the positions are still green rather than after they are not.

The broader lesson is one every investor relearns periodically. The most dangerous moment in a portfolio is not when it is down. It is when it is up for reasons you did not earn, because that is when you stop interrogating the inputs.

Letter 002 · 31 July 2026

Reading the issuer's accounts

Anyone who owns sealed trading card product owns an asset whose supply is set by a public company with a quarterly earnings target. That company files its accounts every three months. It is remarkable how few people in this market read them.

Hasbro reported the second quarter on 21 July, and the numbers were extraordinary. Magic revenue rose 32% to 545.3 million dollars, the first quarter above half a billion in the game's history, driven by what the company described as a record-breaking debut for Marvel Super Heroes. The Wizards of the Coast and Digital Gaming segment grew 27% to 663.8 million. Through six months the segment is up 27% to 1.246 billion, and management raised full-year guidance on the strength of it. This follows a 2025 in which Magic revenue reached 1.72 billion dollars, up roughly 59% year over year, on the back of the Final Fantasy crossover.

A collector reads those numbers and feels validated. The game is healthy, the audience is growing, the brand is stronger than ever. All true, and all beside the point. Every dollar of that revenue is a dollar of product that was manufactured and sold. Record revenue is record supply. For a holder of sealed inventory, the issuer's income statement is not a demand signal. It is a supply schedule, and it is running at the fastest rate in the history of the asset class.

There is a second line worth reading. Wizards posted a 40.7% operating margin in the quarter against an adjusted 46.3% a year earlier, with the compression attributed to product development and marketing spend. That is a company investing to sustain the cadence rather than harvesting a windfall. The chief executive talked about line of sight to continued growth in 2027. Translate that into supply terms and it says the release schedule is a strategy, not an accident, and it is not going to slow down because the internet is tired.

The schedule itself makes the point. 2026 carries seven sets, four of them Universes Beyond and three in-universe, the first year in which crossovers outnumber original Magic worlds. Lorwyn Eclipsed opened in January, Teenage Mutant Ninja Turtles followed in March, Secrets of Strixhaven in April, Marvel Super Heroes in June, The Hobbit in August, with Reality Fracture and Star Trek still to come. Wizards' head designer has called the year an outlier and signalled a return to three in-universe and three crossover sets. I would treat that as a statement of intent rather than a commitment. The 2026 calendar exists because the 2025 numbers justified it, and the 2026 numbers are better.

What does this mean for how the book is positioned? It sharpens the distinction that matters most. There are two kinds of scarcity in this market and they are not the same asset. The first is scarcity by exhaustion, where the print run is closed and the only remaining question is how many survive. The second is scarcity by restraint, where supply is finite only because the publisher has chosen not to add more. The first is a fact. The second is a forecast about someone else's incentives, and this quarter's accounts tell you exactly what those incentives are.

Practically, that argues for the vintage and out-of-print sleeve carrying more of the book than the crossover sleeve, not less, even though the crossover sleeve is where the exciting price action lives. Today the split is roughly 41% vintage and legacy against 34% in a single Tolkien set. I am not comfortable with that ratio, and the discomfort has nothing to do with The Hobbit's quality. It has to do with which half of the book depends on a forecast.

One closing observation. There is a version of this market where the flywheel keeps turning, the audience keeps expanding, and rising demand outruns rising supply for years. That version is entirely possible and it has been the base case since 2020. But it is a bet on demand growth, not a bet on scarcity, and those are different trades with different risks. If you own sealed product because you believe in scarcity, and the thing actually carrying your returns is demand growth, you should at least know that you have changed strategies without deciding to.

Letter 001 · 30 June 2026

The machine that prints the asset

Before writing about positions it is worth writing down how the machine works, because most disagreements about this market turn out to be disagreements about mechanics rather than about value.

Sealed trading card product sits at the intersection of two opposing flows. On one side, supply is created by a single publisher at a rate it chooses, in quantities it does not disclose. On the other, supply is destroyed by consumption, because every box that gets opened permanently leaves the sealed float. Those two flows set the price of everything in this book. Demand matters, obviously, but demand is the input everyone already watches. The supply side is where the mispricings live, because it is opaque on one end and voluntary on the other.

The destruction side has an unusual property. Most collectibles lose supply slowly and involuntarily, through damage, loss and time. Sealed product loses supply quickly and by choice, and the choice is made by exactly the people whose enthusiasm supports the price. When a set is exciting, people open it. The float contracts fastest precisely when the bid is strongest, and it stops contracting when interest fades. This is reflexive in the ordinary sense of the word, and it cuts both directions. A set everyone loves eats its own supply. A set nobody cares about keeps its supply forever, which is why unloved sealed product from an unloved set is rarely the bargain it appears to be. Scarcity without an audience is just inventory.

The creation side is the harder problem. Print runs are set by a company that is measured on quarterly revenue, and the demonstrated pattern is that when demand surprises to the upside, supply follows. Final Fantasy is the case study. Hasbro's own framing was that the Lord of the Rings crossover took about six months to generate 200 million dollars of revenue and Final Fantasy did the same in a single day. The print run was raised repeatedly and the market was still short. That is a company behaving rationally. It is also a company demonstrating, in public, that it will not protect the scarcity of anything it can still print.

Which is why the Reserved List remains the most important structural feature in this entire market, and why the calmest half of it sits in cards printed thirty years ago. The Reserved List is a commitment device: a published promise not to reprint a specific set of older cards, honoured for three decades. It converts scarcity-by-restraint into scarcity-by-fact. Everything anchored to it, from the high-grade Power Nine down to played Revised dual lands, has behaved less like a speculation and more like a store of value. Nothing about that is exciting, and that is the entire attraction.

Where does that leave crossover product, which is the fashionable end of the market and a large part of this book? The optimistic argument is that a licence constrains the publisher in a way that its own intellectual property does not: Wizards cannot simply reprint a Tolkien set whenever it wants, because the rights are not theirs. There is something to this, but less than holders would like. The Lord of the Rings set arrived in 2023 and The Hobbit arrives in 2026 from the same corner of the same universe. The licence did not prevent a second bite; it merely determined the shape of it. Anyone who bought Tales of Middle-earth in 2023 believing they owned the only Tolkien Magic product that would ever exist has now learned what that assumption was worth. The correct way to hold licensed crossover product is as a bet on the durability of an audience, not as a bet on a closed print run.

So the framework for this book comes down to a single question asked of every position: is the scarcity here a fact or a forecast? Out-of-print material from the 1990s is a fact. A crossover set released this year is a forecast about a publisher's restraint and a licensor's appetite. Both can belong in a portfolio. They should not be confused with each other, and they should not be sized as though they carry the same risk.

Everything else, including which specific boxes to own, is downstream of that.
