Letter 020 · 30 August 2026 · Miami, Florida

What I would have to believe

A view and a position are not the same animal, and confusing them is the cheapest mistake in this business.

A view costs nothing. It can be held loosely, revised in private, softened over dinner, quietly abandoned when the facts turn. Nobody audits a view. A position is a view with money and a date stapled to it, and it has a property the view does not: it can be wrong in a way that takes something from you. That asymmetry is the whole reason to write these letters down. Written views become slightly more like positions, because they can be checked later by somebody who remembers.

This is the twentieth and last letter of the sequence, and I am not going to spend it summarising the other nineteen. A summary is a comfortable thing to write and a useless thing to read. What I want to do instead is harder and, I think, the only exercise worth doing at the end of a series like this: state the beliefs this book is actually long, one at a time, with the money attached, and then say what would have to happen for me to admit each one was wrong.

Keynes is supposed to have said, "When the facts change, I change my mind." The line is quoted approvingly by people who never specify, in advance, which facts. That is the part that matters. Deciding what would falsify you afterwards is not falsification, it is editing.

belief one: that old cardboard stays scarce and stays wanted

Roughly 41% of this book sits in vintage and legacy sealed product, most of it acquired on a single day. On 13 May 2026 I put about 1,952 dollars to work across six positions. The largest five: Antiquities at 535.00, an Urza's Saga Tournament Pack at 481.48, Revised at 300.66, Urza's Saga at 288.89, Urza's Legacy at 147.93. It was the busiest day in the fund's history and it was, in effect, one decision written six ways.

People describe that as a bet on scarcity. It is not. It is two bets wearing one coat.

The first is that the supply stays closed. That one I am comfortable with, and it is the closest thing to a fact this market offers. The print runs ended three decades ago and the Reserved List is a published promise not to reprint, honoured since before most of the current audience was born. Whatever you think of the policy, it converts scarcity by restraint into scarcity by fact, and there is nothing else in this asset class that does that.

The second bet is that somebody still wants the stuff, and nothing protects that leg at all. My archive is fairly brutal on the point. A thread from October 2025 asks whether older booster boxes are simply dead shelf product now, and it is not a stupid question. There is a thread from March 2017 observing that after a reprint, a set becomes one of the least valuable things you can open. Scarcity without an audience is inventory, and inventory that nobody is asking about does not announce its own decline. It just stops printing sales.

So what would make me fold? Not a bad month, and not a soft listing. Listings on thin vintage product are decoration. What would do it is completed sales: if, once this book marks on a sold-price basis, the vintage bucket prints realised transactions below cost across two consecutive review cycles while the listed prices stay high, then the bid has gone and only the asking price is still standing there. Wide and widening spreads between what is asked and what is paid are how this asset class dies, quietly, without a single dramatic day.

belief two: that a licence is worth more than the cardboard inside it

The Hobbit is 2,853.54 dollars of cost, 37 units, about 34.8% of net asset value and the largest thematic exposure in the fund. That is not a tilt. That is a statement.

The statement is that a licensed crossover set holds a durable premium over the cost of the product it is printed on. Here is that belief with the arithmetic exposed. Wizards priced the Collector Booster at 37.99, which puts a twelve-pack box at 455.88 at retail. On 18 July 2026 I paid 839.85 for a Collector Booster Display, which is roughly eighty percent over the cost of the cardboard inside it, and I paid it between the end of preview season and the release date, which the record says is the worst window in the calendar to buy anything.

I have written about that purchase before and I am not going to soften it now. What I will do is say what would settle it. The candidate support is a chase card, the Gleaming Gold Smaug, at roughly 500 copies and appearing in under 1% of Collector Boosters. That is the Final Fantasy shape, where a genuinely scarce card in sealed product held the whole set up. The alternative shape is Spider-Man and Avatar, both bid toward 1,000 in presale and both settling back to roughly 750 and 525 once boxes were on shelves.

The Hobbit block gets reviewed in October against post-release prices, and I put that date in print before I knew what it would say, which was the point. If the post-release path tracks the Spider-Man and Avatar shape rather than the Final Fantasy one, then the premium I paid was for an expectation and not for a card, and the correct response is to write it down, stop adding to the block entirely, and say plainly in October that a third of the book was sized on a forecast that did not arrive. Not to average down. Averaging down into a supply event is how a position becomes an identity.

belief three: that the marks mean anything

Every number I have quoted you rests on a belief so quiet it barely feels like one: that public marks approximate what could actually be realised.

This one has already been falsified once, in print, at my expense. On 8 August 2026 I bought a sealed Fallen Empires booster box for 534.99. The book carries it at 543.92. Recent completed sales for the same product printed in the 365 to 386 range. Listed prices and sold prices are different numbers, and on thin vintage sealed the gap can be a third of the value. There is no interpretation of that which flatters me.

There is a second, subtler version of the same problem sitting in the current sheet. Every position acquired since 1 August 2026 is marked between 1.7% and 2.0%. Read that again. It is not a market observation, it is an artifact of a straight-line model filling in the space between marks, and if you saw that band of returns in anybody else's book you would ask what the pricing source was. The honest description is that the most recently bought slice of this portfolio is carried at a number the model produced, not at a number anybody paid.

The fold condition here is not conditional, it is a commitment already made: valuation moves to a sold-price basis wherever completed sales exist. What I have not said before is what I expect that to do. I expect it to take the reported return down, possibly through cost. If it does, that number gets published exactly as it comes out, in the same place and the same size as the 4.62% I have been reporting all year. A fund that only publishes the marking method that flatters it does not have a marking method.

belief four: that waiting pays

The fourth belief is 2,227.02 dollars in cash, about 21% of total assets, earning nothing while the book it sits beside is marked up.

I argued two days ago that this is the most offensive asset in the fund rather than a defensive one, and I stand by the argument. But it is a belief like the others and it needs a way to be wrong. Cash held with a plan is patience. Cash held without one is a man who has run out of ideas and would prefer that to look deliberate. The two are indistinguishable from the outside and, for longer than I would like to admit, from the inside.

So here is the test I will accept. If I buy anything inside the thirty days before a release date, the rule was never a rule and the cash was never patience, it was appetite waiting for an excuse. And the reverse test, which is the one nobody sets themselves: if the dated windows come and go through the rest of 2026, Reality Fracture and Star Trek and the October Hobbit review, and this balance is still sitting here untouched at the end of it, then I am not waiting for prices. I am just afraid, and afraid is not a strategy with a positive expected return.

what none of this has earned

Now the part that undercuts everything above, which is why it goes last rather than being buried in the middle where nobody finishes.

Roughly 96% of this book's cost basis was deployed after 1 May 2026. Four months. Nothing has ever been sold, not one unit, so not a single mark in this fund has been tested against a buyer. All 41 positions are green. Every bucket is positive. The oldest holding, six sleeved packs bought off a shelf for 51.30 on 6 June 2024, is up 64.4% and remains the best performer in the book, which tells you something honest about how much of this outcome was analysis and how much was a man wandering through a store.

A book where everything is green and nothing has been sold has earned exactly no right to confidence. The unrealized 4.62% is not a track record. It is a screenshot. Track records are made of exits, and this fund has never had one. When somebody eventually sends me a price that is below my mark and I have to decide in an afternoon whether my thesis or the buyer is wrong, that will be the first real data point in twenty letters of writing.

Which leaves one thing worth saying to whoever reads this in ten years, possibly me. You will not remember which of these four beliefs turned out to be right. You will remember whether I told you in advance how I would know.

Letter 019 · 29 August 2026 · Miami, Florida

Everything I own is green

There is a particular pleasure in opening a spreadsheet where every line is green. I have forty-one positions in this book, seventy-six sealed units, five buckets, and as of this week every single one of them is marked above what I paid. The book shows 8,063.68 of invested capital carried at 8,436.55. Up 4.62%. Nothing red, nothing to explain, nothing to apologise for at the bottom of a letter.

I looked at that page for a while last week and enjoyed it, and then a small unpleasant thought arrived, which is the thought that should always arrive: what would it take for this page to look exactly like this if I were completely wrong about everything?

Not much, as it turns out. That is what this letter is about.

three reasons the page is green

The first is timing. Roughly 96% of the cost basis in this book was deployed after 1 May 2026. The first purchase was in June 2024 and the book formally began in November 2024, so on paper it has a two-year history, but the money does not. In money terms this is a three-month-old portfolio wearing a two-year-old coat. A three-month-old portfolio marked by any model with a positive drift will be green. That is not a finding. That is a definition.

The second is the marks themselves. They come from public retail and marketplace data, which includes listings as well as completed sales. A listing is an ask. An ask is somebody's opinion about what their box is worth, published by a person with an obvious interest in that opinion being high, and in thin markets the asks sit well above the trades because the sellers who would take less have already sold and left. When I mark to a mixture of asks and trades, I am marking to a number that is systematically friendly to me.

The third is the simplest and the worst. Nothing in this book has ever been sold. Not one unit, not one pack, in two years. Every gain on that page is a gain I have asserted and nobody has yet disputed with money.

the tight band

Now the part I did not spell out clearly enough in earlier letters, and should have.

Every position I have acquired since 1 August is marked between plus 1.7% and plus 2.0%. Every one. That covers a Fifth Edition starter deck bought at 141.23, a sealed Fallen Empires booster box at 534.99, an Ice Age starter deck from 1995 at 207.58, and a modern Hobbit Collector Booster Box at 795.00. Four products from four separate eras of printing, bought through two different channels, with entirely different buyers, entirely different float and entirely different reasons for anyone to want them. And they agree with each other, to within about thirty basis points.

No market does that. Arithmetic does that.

The mechanism is not hidden, I built it: the net asset value path between marks is straight-line modelled and there are no intramonth marks. Which means a recent position's reported return is largely a function of how many days ago I bought it. It is a clock, not a price. When I look at that band of 1.7 to 2.0 I am not observing four assets appreciating in unison. I am observing my own spreadsheet counting.

And it is not only August. Go back to 13 May, the busiest day this book has ever had, when roughly 1,952 dollars went out across six positions. Five of those were vintage. An Antiquities booster pack at 535.00 carried at 561.83. An Urza's Saga Tournament Pack at 481.48 carried at 505.63. A Revised booster pack at 300.66 carried at 315.74. An Urza's Saga booster pack at 288.89 carried at 303.38. An Urza's Legacy pack at 147.93 carried at 155.35. Every one of them marked at exactly plus 5.0%. Four different sets, printed years apart, with different survivorship, different collector bases and different reasons to be scarce, all appreciating by precisely the same percentage to the first decimal place.

If a strategist showed me a portfolio like that in my actual job I would ask one question and it would not be about the assets.

the position that proves it

I have written before about the Fallen Empires box, and I am going to keep writing about it until the accounting changes, because it is the cleanest test of the method I own.

Bought 8 August at 534.99. Carried at 543.92, which is to say plus 1.7%, which is to say inside the band, which is to say the model. Meanwhile recent completed sales on the same marketplace for the same product printed in the 365 to 386 range. If those completed sales are the price, I am not up 1.7% on that box. I am down somewhere between a quarter and a third of it.

Both numbers cannot be true. One of them came from what people asked. The other came from what people paid.

Here is the part I glossed over the first time. If the gap is real on that position, the gap is not a property of that position. It is a property of the method, and the method has no way of knowing which of the other forty positions it is currently wrong about. I found this one because I went looking on a box I felt uneasy about. Unease is not a screening process. It is the opposite of one: it finds exactly the errors you were already half expecting and leaves the rest sitting there in green.

Two changes are committed. Valuation moves to a sold-price basis wherever completed sales exist. And the Hobbit block, which is about 34.8% of the book, gets reviewed against post-release prices in October rather than against listings written by people who own it.

the part of the truth I do not publish

One more omission, since I am cataloguing them. The portfolio page shows the invested book. It does not show the cash. There is 2,227.02 sitting in cash, about 21% of total assets, and it has earned nothing and appreciated by nothing and appears nowhere on the page a reader sees. So the return this book advertises is the return on the part of me that was invested and happened to be right, presented without the part of me that was waiting.

Contrast all this with the three positions I actually trust. A six-pack of Lord of the Rings sleeved set boosters bought off a shelf in June 2024 at 51.30, carried at 84.36. A starter kit at 21.39, carried at 32.13. A scene box at 23.99, carried at 35.45. Up 64.4%, 50.2% and 47.8%. What makes those marks credible is not that the method changed. It is that time has had a chance to disagree with them and has not. Two years, a full release cycle, a sequel announcement, and the numbers held. A mark you have carried through events is worth more than a mark you have carried through a week.

And that is the actual lesson, which has very little to do with cardboard. The dangerous moment in any book is not the drawdown. A drawdown makes you check everything: the inputs, the sizing, the thesis, the man who sold it to you. Nobody has ever lost money because they audited too aggressively while losing. The dangerous moment is the one I am in, where the number is pleasant and the pleasantness is manufactured by an assumption nobody has stress tested, including me, especially me.

Marks put the working discipline about as briefly as it can be put: you can't predict, you can prepare. I would add a smaller and less elegant rule for personal use. When the page is green, go and find out why before someone else does.

Because I am not up 4.62%. I am up 4.62% according to me, and so far there is nobody on the other side of that sentence.

Letter 018 · 28 August 2026 · Miami, Florida

The cash is a position

The most interesting line in a portfolio is almost always the one that is not on the page. I have believed this for years about other people's books and I have been slow to apply it to my own.

There is a thread in my archive from October 2025 in which someone lays out their Magic holdings as a financial portfolio, item by item, with what was paid and what it is supposedly worth. It is a serious piece of work and I recognised the impulse behind it immediately, because it is the same impulse that produced this fund. What I noticed, reading it, is what I notice in every accounting of this kind, including the ones I have written. It is a list of purchases. The money that was not spent does not appear anywhere, because money that was not spent does not have a picture next to it.

My own portfolio page has the same shape. It reports 41 positions, 76 sealed units, five strategy buckets, 8,063.68 dollars of invested capital marked at 8,436.55, up 4.62% and nothing ever sold. All of that is true and all of it is incomplete. The ledger behind the page has a forty-second line the website does not report: 2,227.02 dollars in cash, sitting beside a book of 8,436.55. Call it 21% of total assets.

That is a large number to leave unexplained, and an unexplained large cash balance is read by everybody, correctly, as indecision. So this letter is the explanation. I would rather defend the cash than have it look like a man who could not make up his mind.

what cash is for, and what it is not for here

In an ordinary fund, cash does two jobs at once and they get conflated. The first job is defensive plumbing. You hold cash because investors can ask for their money back, because a clearing house can ask for margin, because the fee bill arrives whether or not the year went well, because a fund that is forced to sell something in order to pay for something else has already lost the argument before it starts. That cash is not an investment decision. It is a liability sitting on the asset side of the page, waiting to be claimed by somebody.

The second job is the one everybody talks about and almost nobody is actually free to do: the cash exists to be spent at a price somebody else sets in a moment of stress.

Now look at this book. There is no outside capital, so nobody can redeem. There is no leverage, so nobody can call me. There are no fees, so there is no bill arriving in January. The product sits in my own custody, so there is no counterparty who can decide to be difficult on a bad week. Every structural reason to hold cash defensively has been removed, not by cleverness but by the accident of the book being small and self-funded.

Which means the 2,227.02 is not a buffer. It is not prudence, it is not risk management, it is not dry powder in the apologetic sense people use that phrase when they have missed a rally. It is the most purely offensive asset in the fund. It is the only line in the ledger that can be pointed at anything, at any price, on any day, without asking a single other position for permission. Everything else I own has already committed to an opinion. The cash has not, and that is the entire point of it.

I want to put that the other way round too, because it is easy to nod at and hard to hold on to. Every dollar of cash is a dollar that has declined to buy something. That is a real decision with a real cost, taken 41 times over by not becoming a forty-second position. If that decision is never exercised, it was not patience. It was avoidance wearing patience as a costume.

the rule and the money only work as a pair

In an earlier letter I committed to a hard rule: no acquisition inside the thirty days before a set's release date. That window, between the end of preview season and the day product reaches shelves, is where I have made my most expensive mistake, and I put the rule in writing precisely so that it would be embarrassing to break.

Here is the part I did not say at the time. A rule like that is worth nothing on its own.

Consider a buyer who is fully invested and holds no cash. He can announce any restriction he likes about not buying into a release, and it costs him nothing, because he could not have bought anyway. He has not written down a rule. He has written down an opinion, and opinions that cannot be tested are free. Now consider the opposite man, holding a fifth of his assets in cash with no rule at all. Every presale, every countdown, every thread telling him supply will be tight at release is aimed directly at him, and he has the means to act on all of it. He has not written down a rule either. He has written down a temptation.

The cash makes the rule expensive, which is what makes it a rule. The rule makes the cash survive contact with a release calendar, which is what makes it cash. Neither half functions alone, and I do not think I understood that when I wrote either of them down.

what it is waiting for

Waiting is only respectable if you can say what you are waiting for. Otherwise it is a mood.

What I am waiting for is a pattern I have now watched twice. Collector Booster boxes for the Spider-Man and Avatar crossovers were bid toward 1,000 dollars in presale and settled back to roughly 750 and 525 once product actually reached shelves. Not a crash, not a scandal, just the ordinary deflation of a price set by people who could not yet buy the thing, replaced by a price set by people who could. Final Fantasy went the other way and recovered strongly, on the back of a chase card that stayed genuinely scarce in sealed product. Two outcomes, and the difference between them is knowable after release and mostly unknowable before it.

The calendar is the gift here. 2026 carries seven sets, with Reality Fracture and Star Trek still to come, and the Hobbit block faces its own review against post-release prices in October. I do not know what any of those will do. I do know when they happen. An asset class that schedules its supply events in advance is handing a patient buyer a series of dated windows, and the only thing required to be there is money that has not already been spent.

the honest cost

I owe you the other side of this, because a letter that only argues for its own conclusion is advertising.

Cash underperforms in a rising book and outperforms in a falling one. That is arithmetic, not a forecast, and it does not become insight by being said confidently. The book has been rising. All 41 positions are marked above cost, the fund shows 4.62% unrealized, and every dollar parked in cash across those months earned none of it. If the next year looks like the last few months, the cash will have cost me real return and this letter will read as a rationalisation. I accept that. I would rather be caught defending an idle fifth of the book than caught explaining why I bought at a presale price with borrowed conviction.

What justifies the drag is not a market view. It is the structure of the thing. Seven scheduled supply events a year, each one a public countdown followed by a price discovered in daylight, is an unusual gift to somebody who is under no obligation to participate. Most investors pay for optionality. Here it is free, and the only price of admission is being liquid on a date somebody else chose.

The 41 positions are what I have decided. The forty-second line is what I have refused to decide yet, and on a long enough calendar that refusal is the position I expect to be proudest of.

Letter 017 · 26 August 2026 · Miami, Florida

Dead shelf product

You have seen the shelf I mean. It runs above the display case at the back of a game shop, higher than anyone reaches without asking, and on it sit four or five booster boxes from sets nobody has drafted in a decade. The shrink wrap has gone slightly cloudy. There is a price written on a sticker in handwriting, and the handwriting is older than the sticker adhesive, which has yellowed. The owner will tell you, if you ask, that he is not in a hurry on those.

The buyer's instinct in that moment is to read the sticker as strength. The price never came down. Nobody had to cut. Whatever the market did to everything else, these held. That reading is almost exactly backwards, and the reason is simple enough to state in one line: a price that has not moved in four years is not a price that held. It is the absence of a trade.

A thread in my archive from October 2025 asks the question directly, whether older booster boxes at this point are just dead shelf product. I have been circling that thread for months. It is the counterweight to nearly everything else I have written in these letters, and I would rather handle it than hope no one brings it up.

the same fact, read twice

The case for vintage sealed is a single sentence: nobody can print more. I have leaned on that sentence repeatedly. The Reserved List is a published promise not to reprint a specific set of older cards, honoured for three decades, and it does the one thing nothing else in this market does. It converts scarcity from a forecast about a company's restraint into a fact about the world. When I say the calm part of this book sits in cardboard from the 1990s, that promise is what I am pointing at.

Here is the problem. Read that sentence again and notice that it says nothing whatsoever about anybody wanting the thing.

Fixed supply is a statement about the numerator. Every reason to own the asset lives in the denominator, and the denominator is a population of human beings who have to keep caring. The property that makes vintage sealed safe, that the float can never grow, is the identical property that can make it impossible to sell, because a float that never grows is also a float that never refreshes its audience. New product recruits. Old product waits. The thing that protects you from dilution is the same thing that leaves you dependent on a bid you do not control and cannot create.

I wrote in the first of these letters that scarcity without an audience is just inventory. I put it there deliberately, at the front, so that I could not later pretend I had not seen it coming. This letter is that sentence pointed at my own book.

the archive has a shape, and the shape is the argument

I keep an archive of 316 forum threads about this market, running from 2011 to this year, pulled from four public communities. I assembled it in August 2026 to have something other than my own memory to argue with. What I did not expect was that the distribution of the threads would turn out to be more informative than most of their contents.

Count them by year. One thread from 2011. Five from 2013. Seven from 2014. Then 26 from 2015, back down to 10 in 2016, 12 in 2020, 15 in 2021, 25 in 2023, 31 in 2025, and 119 from 2026 alone. Thin in the middle, thick at both ends, with more written this year than in the previous decade combined.

Some of that is how the archive was gathered. A collection assembled in 2026 will always over-sample 2026, because recent threads are easier to find and easier to reach, and I do not want to dress a collection artifact up as a discovery. But not all of it is that. The 2015 spike is real and it corresponds to a period when people had something specific to be loud about. The quiet years were genuinely quiet.

So here is what the shape says, and it says it about attention rather than about price. Attention in this market is not spread evenly across time. It arrives in bursts, attaches to whatever is new, and then leaves. And the bid travels with the attention, because the bid is made of people who are currently thinking about the thing. In a market with no market makers, no dealers standing between buyer and seller, and no obligation on anyone to quote, the bid is not a structural feature of the asset. It is a weather condition.

A box on a high shelf in a quiet year has not been repriced. It has been forgotten, which looks identical on the sticker and is not remotely the same thing in the hand.

which brings me to 41% of this book

The vintage and legacy sleeve is 3,294.20 of cost carried at 3,427.88, up 4.1%, 21 sealed units, roughly 41% of net asset value. It is the largest thing I own and it is exposed to precisely the risk in this letter.

Take two positions deliberately, and not the flattering ones. An Ice Age Starter Deck from 1995, bought on 8 August at 207.58. A Fifth Edition Starter Deck bought on 1 August at 141.23. Both are as safe from reprinting as anything in this market can be. The print runs closed when the current audience was mostly not yet born. No committee can vote to make more of them and no licensor can be persuaded to allow a second bite. On the supply side these are as close to a fact as I will ever own.

And both of them are, entirely and without any hedge I can construct, a bet that in ten years somebody still cares. Not that somebody still plays the game, which is a different and easier bet. That somebody specifically wants a sealed starter deck from 1995 badly enough to send a stranger two hundred dollars for it. I cannot manufacture that person. I cannot even count them today.

I already have the evidence of what that costs when the bid is thin, and I have published it. The Fallen Empires box I bought this month at 534.99 sits on my sheet at 543.92, while completed sales of the same product printed between 365 and 386. That gap is not a pricing quirk. That gap is the illiquidity, quantified, showing up in the only place it ever shows up, which is the distance between what people ask and what people pay. On thin vintage sealed it can run to a third of the value. My marks move to a sold-price basis wherever completed sales exist, which will make several of these numbers worse and all of them truer.

The archive knows this too, in its own vocabulary. A thread from February 2023 asks how many people are trapped holding sealed collector product. A thread from December 2025 is titled simply Junk Wax, which is the sports card analogy, and which everyone in this market understands instantly and nobody enjoys. Junk wax was never a story about fraud or about a bad product. It was a story about a generation that stopped showing up.

Howard Marks has a line I keep returning to: being too far ahead of your time is indistinguishable from being wrong. In illiquid assets the two are also indistinguishable from each other for a very long time, which is the part that gets people. You cannot tell the difference between an asset that is early and an asset that is over, because both of them sit there quietly and do not trade. The scarcity story is comfortable exactly because it is unfalsifiable in the short run. Nothing happening is consistent with both outcomes.

None of which makes the vintage sleeve a mistake. It makes it a position with one leg standing on a fact and the other standing on a hope, and I would rather say that plainly than let the strength of the first leg do the work of the second.

The Reserved List guarantees the supply will never grow. Nobody has ever written a list that guarantees the demand.

Letter 016 · 24 August 2026 · Miami, Florida

Twenty-eight packs from a shelf in Miami

On the afternoon of 23 August I stood in a Target in Miami holding twenty-eight sealed booster packs against my chest, because I had not thought to pick up a basket on the way in and was too far down the aisle to go back for one. Two of them slid loose and I crouched to collect them off the floor. A woman waited, politely, for me to stop blocking the puzzles. The self-checkout took a few minutes because the machine wanted an adult to confirm that a stack of cardboard was, in fact, a stack of cardboard. The receipt said 239.40.

That works out to 8.55 a pack, and I want to be precise about how strange the next sentence is. It is the cleanest execution in this book. Forty-one positions, some of them assembled with real care over weeks of watching, and the best price I have ever paid for anything was paid in fluorescent light between the puzzles and the party supplies, in shorts, dropping product on the floor.

Nothing about it looked like investing. I have come to think that is the whole content of the letter, and everything below is me trying to explain why.

the price is a stack of decisions

When you buy anything in this market you are not paying a price. You are paying the last one in a chain, and the chain is longer than most buyers ever have reason to count.

It starts with the publisher, which sets a number. For The Hobbit, the Collector Booster was priced at 37.99, which makes a twelve-pack box 455.88 at the top of the stack. That number is the only one in the entire chain set by the party that actually knows how much product exists. Everything after it is somebody else's guess dressed as a decision.

Next come the distributors, who do not sell to you and whose behaviour you will never observe directly. You observe its shadow. My archive carries 73 threads tagged for distributor allocations and wholesale supply, the third largest theme in the whole collection. People do not write seventy-three threads about allocation when allocation is working.

Then the shops, and here the archive is unusually good, because it caught the same subject from three different chairs across eight years. A thread from January 2014 is somebody explaining, patiently and at length, why stores charge more than the suggested price on the box. A thread from July 2019 has a shop owner explaining a round of price increases from the other side of the counter. A thread from July 2022 is about local shops in trouble and what was sitting in their inventories while they were in trouble. Put those three in order and you have the entire retail layer: the customer who thinks the printed price is the price, the retailer explaining that it never was, and the moment when a struggling retailer's inventory stops being a source of demand and becomes a source of supply. The archive has 53 threads tagged retail dumps and another 53 on flash sales and cycle bottoms, which is what that moment looks like from the buyer's side.

Then the aggregators, which take all of the above and report a blended number. A blend is a real number about no transaction. I have already written about what that costs me. On 8 August I paid 534.99 for a sealed Fallen Empires box and I carry it at 543.92, while recent completed sales of the same product printed between 365 and 386. Listed prices and sold prices are different numbers, and on thin vintage sealed the gap between them can be a third of the value. That is not a marking error I discovered by being clever. It is the ordinary condition of a market where the reported price is an average of asks.

And at the end of it, the collector pays whatever the thinnest layer says. Not the deepest layer, not the best informed one. The thinnest. Whichever seller happened to be pointing at him when he decided he wanted the thing today.

what a shelf price actually is

Set that chain against the shelf in Miami. The price was posted before I arrived. It was the same number for everyone in the county that afternoon. It did not move because I looked eager, it did not carry a feedback score, it did not arrive with a photograph of one corner taken at a generous angle, it had no shipping attached and no counterparty whose reputation I had to price. A national retailer sets a number on its own schedule and is slow to change it, and slowness is the feature. There was nothing to negotiate and nobody to be outsmarted by.

Which produces a result I find genuinely funny. The most sophisticated venues in this market, the ones with live data and real price discovery and thousands of participants, are the ones where I pay the widest spread. The least sophisticated venue, a big-box store that sells this product next to the birthday candles and does not care what it is worth, gave me the tightest. Retail is the last honest price in this market, and it is honest precisely because the seller is not paying attention.

There is a small echo here I noticed only afterward. The first thing this book ever bought, in June 2024, was six sleeved set booster packs from a large electronics retailer for 51.30. It is still the best performing position I own, marked up 64.4%. Same format, same kind of seller, two years apart. I would not build a philosophy on a sample of two. I would not ignore it either.

cheap execution of a questionable idea

Now the part I like less, and I would rather write it myself than have it pointed out to me.

What I bought at that excellent price was the most abundant format of the newest set on the shelf. Sleeved set boosters of a current release are the opposite of scarce. They are printed to meet demand that the publisher can still observe, they sit in every large store in the country, and if they sell well the answer is more of them. Letter 004 was explicitly a warning against exactly this: buying the plentiful end of the newest product because it is available and affordable, and mistaking availability for opportunity. I wrote that in June. I bought twenty-eight of them in August.

The size of it makes the point sharper. The Hobbit block is the largest thematic exposure in the book, 2,853.54 of cost and roughly 35% of the book at cost, spread across 37 sealed units. Of those 37 units, 28 are these packs. By count, my biggest bet is now mostly composed of the least scarce thing I own. By money it is nothing like that, because the value sits in a Collector Booster Display bought at 839.85 and a Collector Booster Box at 795.00. Both readings are true. The unit count is the one that would embarrass me in front of a stranger, and I think the embarrassment is informative.

A reader might ask what the position has done. It is carried at 244.16, up 2.0%. Please treat that as noise. Every position I have acquired since 1 August is marked between +1.7% and +2.0%, which is an artifact of a straight-line mark model between valuation dates, not an observation about anything. The number is a placeholder wearing a decimal point.

In its favour: it did comply with the thirty-day rule I committed to in print, since a set that is already on a Target shelf is a set that has already been released. That was the whole point of the rule, to stop me buying into the window between preview season and release when the marketing is loudest and the supply has not yet arrived. It worked. And the Hobbit block gets reviewed against post-release prices in October, at which point I will know something about this that I do not know now.

Buffett has the line, borrowed from Graham: price is what you pay, value is what you get. It is quoted so often that people have stopped hearing the second half. I paid the best price in the book. That settles the first clause completely and the second clause not at all.

A good fill is not an argument. It is only the part of a bad idea that cannot be blamed later.

Letter 015 · 21 August 2026 · Miami, Florida

Who is on the other side

Every sealed box in this book is a promise. The promise is quite specific: that inside the shrink wrap there are the correct number of packs, from the correct set, printed in the year the label says, untouched since a machine in a factory closed them. I have never verified that promise on a single unit I own. Neither has anyone else in the chain. The promise has simply been passed along, from whoever made it to whoever believed it, and I am the current holder of the belief.

So the question that ought to sit at the front of this entire enterprise is not what the print run was, or what the chase card is, or what the publisher reports next quarter. It is much older and much duller than any of that. Who made the promise, and what happens to them if it turns out to be false?

I have written fifteen letters about supply. This is the first one about the counterparty, which tells you something about my own priorities that I do not love.

the audit that destroys the asset

Start with what makes this asset class genuinely odd, because it is not the cardboard.

Every other thing I have ever held can be checked without being consumed. A bond has a trustee, a coupon record, a registrar somewhere with your name on a list. A share is an entry in a system built specifically so that ownership can be confirmed by a stranger. Even a physical bar of metal can be assayed, and afterwards it is still a bar of metal, slightly smaller and considerably better documented.

A sealed box can be verified in exactly one way. You open it. And when you open it you have destroyed the thing whose authenticity you were confirming, because the seal was the asset. Not the cards. The seal. The audit and the position are mutually exclusive, and there is no version of this where you get both.

Sit with what that implies about price formation. The value is not in the contents. The value is in an unexamined claim about the contents, and every participant in the market knows the claim will almost certainly never be examined, because examining it is the one action that guarantees the value goes away. The whole thing runs on the shared understanding that nobody will check.

It gets worse with time, and in the wrong direction. The older and rarer a sealed box becomes, the more it is worth, and the more it is worth, the more insane it would be to open it, and the less likely anybody ever is to find out. Verification risk compounds in exactly the same place where value compounds. The most expensive box in any collection is definitionally the one least likely to ever be opened, which makes it the one least likely to ever be proved real.

what the archive argues about, and what it does not

My archive runs to 316 threads across fifteen years, tagged by theme. The distribution is worth reading as a document in its own right. Reprint risk and supply shocks, 87 threads. Commander demand, 83. Distributor allocations and wholesale supply, 73. Retail dumps, 53. Flash sales and cycle bottoms, 53. Licensed intellectual property, 44. Bans and format governance, 41. Buyouts and price spikes, 33. Vintage and Reserved List scarcity, 32.

Nine themes. Not one of them is about whether the box is real.

The threads exist, they are simply scattered and unloved. In January 2015 someone posted a warning about being taken for 1,200 dollars on eBay. In August 2017 someone asked, plainly and without much hope, where a person could find sealed sellers who could be trusted. And this month, August 2026, there are warnings circulating that deals which look too good to be true are exactly that, including when they come from sources that appear reputable.

Read those three dates together. Eleven years separate the first from the last, the market grew by an order of magnitude in between, the publisher reported its first half billion dollar quarter in July, and the state of the art in counterparty diligence is still a stranger on a forum saying be careful. Nothing was built. No standard emerged. The market spent fifteen years arguing about how many boxes exist and almost none of it asking whether any given box is one of them.

five doors, and the money went through the weakest one

This book buys through five channels: eBay, Amazon, TCG Marketplace, Best Buy and Target. They are usually treated as interchangeable, as places where the price is. They are not interchangeable at all, and the difference has nothing to do with price.

At one end is the retail shelf. The first purchase this book ever made was six sleeved set booster packs from a national electronics chain in June 2024, for 51.30. The custody chain on that is publisher, distributor, retailer, me. Four steps, all of them corporate, all of them recorded, none of them anonymous. If those packs are wrong, an enormous company with lawyers is wrong, and the recourse is obvious. It is the most verifiable position in the book. It is also one of the smallest.

At the other end is the open marketplace, where the platform is a venue rather than a seller, where the counterparty may be a shop or a person or an estate, and where the object may have spent thirty years in one closet or thirty years across six of them. Here is where the money actually went. The Antiquities booster pack at 535.00, bought on eBay. The Urza's Saga Tournament Pack at 481.48, eBay. The Revised pack at 300.66, the Urza's Saga pack at 288.89, the Urza's Legacy pack at 147.93, all eBay, all on the same day in May. The Fallen Empires box at 534.99, eBay, in August.

The Vintage and Legacy bucket is about 41% of net asset value, 3,294.20 of cost across twenty-one units. It is the oldest material I own, the most valuable per unit, the part I have argued most confidently is anchored to a real and durable scarcity, and the part whose provenance is least documented and least documentable. Most of it was printed before the market existed as a market, and nobody kept the kind of records that would now settle an argument.

So the relationship between verifiability and value in this book is precisely inverted from how you would design it if you were designing it. Where the promise is easiest to check, I have the least money. Where it is impossible to check, I have the most.

what self custody does and does not solve

This book has no outside capital, no fees, no leverage and no redemptions. It holds everything in self custody, sealed, controlled by me. I have written about that as a strength, and it is one. It answers theft, loss, damage, humidity, sunlight, and the risk of sitting on somebody else's balance sheet at the wrong moment.

Every one of those is a storage risk. Not one of them is an authenticity risk. Custody answers the question of who can take the box away from me. It says nothing whatsoever about whether the box was ever what the listing claimed. I have built a careful answer to the second-order problem and left the first-order problem exactly where I found it, which is in the hands of people I have never met and cannot name.

I am not going to pretend I have a solution, because I do not. What I can do is small and mostly obvious: buy at retail where retail exists, prefer sellers with long histories and platforms with real buyer protection, keep the photographs and the records at purchase rather than at the moment of doubt, and stop treating a vintage sealed box as if it carried the same risk profile as a pack lifted off a retail shelf and carried to a till.

And then there is the thing that genuinely bothers me. There is no number for this. I can quote a cost, a mark, a bucket weight, a percentage of net asset value. There is no authenticity spread, no percentile, no probability I could honestly attach. So it appears in none of my tables, and a risk that appears in none of your tables is a risk you have quietly decided is zero.

Every other risk in this book, I can at least go and look at. This one, I would have to destroy the asset to see.

The second bite

On 13 August I paid 795 dollars for a Hobbit Collector Booster Box. It was an ordinary purchase, made the ordinary way, and I felt the ordinary small pleasure of it. Then I went back to the book to record the entry, and the spreadsheet did the thing spreadsheets do, which is to put two facts next to each other that you had been keeping in separate rooms.

The best performing bucket I own is Lord of the Rings: Tales of Middle-earth. It cost me 550.29, it is up strongly, by a wider margin than anything else in the book, and it has been the one line I have quietly enjoyed looking at since I started. Directly underneath it, larger by a factor of four, sits The Hobbit. And I had just added to The Hobbit.

I want to describe that feeling accurately, because I do not think I have had it before in any other market. It is not the feeling of a hedge. A hedge is something you buy on purpose, knowing what it offsets. This was the feeling of realising that the thing I had just bought because I liked it is also, structurally, the thing most likely to take away the number I liked most. I had spent 795 dollars to compete with myself, and I had done it cheerfully.

what the licence was supposed to do

Go back to the argument I made in the first of these letters, because this is where it gets tested.

The publisher can print its own worlds as often as it likes. Nothing restrains it except commercial judgement, and commercial judgement, on the evidence, points at printing more. The optimistic case for the licensed sets was always that borrowed intellectual property behaves differently. The publisher cannot simply decide to run a Tolkien set again next quarter, because the rights are not its own and there is a second party who must agree. A licence, on this reading, is a supply constraint that the in-universe sets do not have.

I believed a softer version of that. I want to be honest about the softness, because the soft version is how most people hold a wrong idea. I did not think a second Tolkien product was impossible. I thought it was distant, and I thought distance was enough.

Here is the flaw, and it is not subtle once you say it out loud. A licence governs who may print. It says almost nothing about how much, or how often, or how soon. Tales of Middle-earth arrived in 2023. The Hobbit arrived in 2026. Same author, same world, adjacent stories, three years apart. Whatever the contract says, it plainly did not say once.

And look at the year around it. 2026 carries seven sets, four of them crossovers and three of them in-universe, the first year in the game's history that borrowed worlds outnumber original ones. The company's own head of design has called 2026 an outlier and signalled a return to three and three. Perhaps. But a market that assumed licensed product was rationed by contract has just watched the ration double, and the correction to the schedule came after the fact, not before it.

what a second bite does to the first one

The cleanest evidence I have on this question is not from crossovers at all. It is from a pair of threads in my archive about a group of lands.

In August 2014, someone tracked dealer prices for the Onslaught fetchlands before and after a reprint was announced. Those cards had not been printed since 2002. They were reprinted in September 2014, and the archive has the prices on both sides of the announcement, which is rarer than it sounds, because most of this market's memory is anecdote.

The better thread is from October 2015. By then every Khans fetchland had reached an all-time high, with one exception: the single land that had been printed a second time in a supplemental product. Five cards. Same set, same rarity, same function, same demand, same formats. Four of them made new highs. The fifth, the one that got a second bite, lagged.

That is about as close to a controlled experiment as this market will ever hand you. Every variable held constant except one, and the one that moved was supply, and the effect was visible in the price of a specific card in a specific month. I do not want to overfit it. A sealed box is not a single card, a new set from the same licence is not a reprint of the same product, and the collector demand for a 2023 Tolkien box does not have to behave like the tournament demand for a land. The direction, though, is the direction. When the market learns that a thing it thought was finished is not finished, it re-rates the thing that already exists, and it does not re-rate it upward.

the thread from May

In May of this year, before any of the Hobbit product existed as an object, my archive picks up a thread about what the Hobbit reveals mean for the Lord of the Rings cards people already own. Note the timing. The set was still being revealed. Nothing had shipped, nothing had been opened, no price had printed. What the thread contains is a group of holders doing the arithmetic in public, in real time, on the value of a position they bought in 2023 under an assumption that was in the process of being cancelled.

This is a sentiment record, not a price record, and I would not lean on it for a mark. But reveal season is exactly when belief gets repriced, and belief is most of what a collector market is made of. Everything after that is confirmation, arriving slowly, at retail.

Howard Marks has a line about this that I think about often: experience is what you get when you didn't get what you wanted. Those 2023 holders are getting experience. So am I. I own 550.29 of cost in the product they are worried about.

on both sides of my own trade

So here is the awkward accounting, stated plainly, because if I do not state it someone else will.

My best performing bucket is 2023 Tolkien, ahead of everything else I own, and it is the product being diluted. My largest thematic exposure is The Hobbit, 2,200.83 of cost across seven units, about 29.7% of the book, and it is the diluting agent. The book is on both sides of its own trade. That sounds balanced. It is not balanced, and the imbalance is the whole point: I have roughly four times as much money in the new thing as in the old one. If a second bite transfers value from 2023 to 2026, I collect it. If it destroys value in both, which is the outcome the 2015 thread does not rule out, I pay for it four times over on one side and once on the other.

And I am not paying retail for the privilege. I have already put in print that I bought a Hobbit Collector Booster Display on 18 July for 839.85, when the publisher priced a Collector Booster at 37.99, which puts twelve of them at 455.88 at the counter. Roughly eighty percent over the cost of the cardboard, bought between the end of preview season and release, which the record says is the worst window there is. Four hundred dollars for a box

You almost never get to hear a market say no. In equities the refusal is silent and it is instant. It is a lower print, and by the time you see the print the argument that produced it is finished and nobody kept minutes. Somewhere a few thousand people decided a number was too high, and the entire record of that decision is a tick. No reasoning, no dissent, no account of who held out and at what level they would have folded. The market speaks in a single syllable and then declines to elaborate.

In July 2023 the archive caught it elaborating. There is a run of threads reacting to a 400 dollar Commander Masters set booster box, and the reaction is disbelief, and it is loud, and somebody wrote it down. That is the thread people remember. But it is the second thread that I keep going back to, the one asking at what price people actually would start buying them.

Think about what that thread is. It is a demand curve being assembled in public, by hand, by the people who constitute it, one reply at a time. Not a survey. Not an estimate produced by somebody with a model and an interest. Buyers voluntarily disclosing their reservation prices to strangers, for free, in advance. I have never seen an equity market permit that, and I do not expect to. You get the clearing price and nothing else, and everyone pretends the clearing price contains the information, when in fact it is the residue of the information after the interesting part has been destroyed.

I should be fair about what that thread is not. The people answering had no obligation to be honest, no money at stake in the answer, and every incentive to talk the number down in front of an audience that wanted it lower. Stated willingness to pay is famously not the same as willingness to pay. If I put this in front of a committee I would be told, correctly, that it is a set of unverified claims by an unrepresentative sample of people who chose to reply. All true. It is still more than any equity market has ever given me, and I would rather work with a noisy record of reasoning than a clean record of outcomes with the reasoning stripped out.

announced and accepted are two events

We use one word, price, for two separate things that happen at two separate times. A price is announced by a seller. A price is accepted by a buyer. Collapsing those into a single noun is convenient and it hides the most important variable in this market, which is the length of time the gap between them can stay open.

During that gap, something odd exists. A product sits on a shelf at a number that nobody has agreed to. It is not overpriced in any sense a spreadsheet can capture, because there is no transaction to compare it against. It is simply an assertion, standing there, waiting. Both sides have reasons to wait. The seller holds the number because the number anchors the next product and the one after that. The buyer waits because waiting is free and shelf space is not.

How does the gap close? Three ways, and only three. The announced price comes down. The resistance comes down, because a chase card appears, or a format shifts, or a scarcity story takes hold. Or the product quietly goes away and the question is never settled. All three happen here regularly, and the archive keeps score: 53 threads tagged Retail Dumps and 53 tagged Flash Sales & Cycle Bottoms. That is a lot of documented gap closing, and it does not close upward very often.

the people already inside the gap

In February 2023, five months before the disbelief threads, somebody asked how many people are trapped holding sealed collector product. The word to sit with is trapped. Not losing. Trapped describes a specific condition: the price is nominally intact, the position is nominally fine, and nobody will meet it. That is the gap seen from the inside. Every announced price that has not yet been accepted has somebody standing behind it who accepted it already, at a moment when the announcement still sounded like a fact.

Then in August 2023 there is a thread titled, in effect, none of this is normal, asking what normal even means for this market any more. Same year. Inside roughly six months the archive moves from disbelief at a number, to counting the people stuck behind it, to asking whether there is a reference point at all. There is also a 2023 thread arguing the game had seen more than nine years' worth of reprints inside a three-year period, which is one answer to the question of what happened to normal.

Galbraith had the shape of this: financial genius is before the fall. He was writing about people, but it works on prices. A number looks like insight while somebody is still willing to pay it.

Wizards priced the Collector Booster at 37.99, so twelve of them cost 455.88 at retail. It closed because the question finally became answerable.

The head designer has called it an outlier and signalled a return to three and three. I take that seriously and I would note what it is. In every market somebody has to be the last one who was excited. Nobody has ever known, at the time, that it was him.

Listed is not sold

On the evening of 8 August 2026 I bought a sealed Fallen Empires Booster Box on eBay for 534.99. Then, with the confirmation already sitting in my inbox and the money already gone, I went back to the same search and did the one thing I had not done beforehand. I filtered it to completed sales. I want to stay on the ordering for a moment, because the ordering is the whole story and the number is only the receipt. It is not that I did not know how to check completed sales. It is a filter. It is that a check performed before the purchase is a question, and a check performed after the purchase is a formality, and I chose the formality. Then the book made it worse, politely and automatically. The position is carried at 543.92, because the straight-line mark model marked it up, as it marks everything up. why this one stings

I have already put a worse dollar mistake in print. The Hobbit Collector Booster Display, bought at 839.85 on 18 July 2026 against a retail cost of 455.88, is a larger error in absolute terms and I have said so. But this one bothers me more, and I have been trying to work out why.

It is because vintage was supposed to be the safe half. It is the part I described as scarcity-by-fact rather than scarcity-by-forecast, the calm end, the section that exists precisely so that the fashionable end can be wrong without taking the whole thing down with it. Mistakes in the crossover bucket are priced in. I expect them. Mistakes in the vintage bucket are a different category, because the reason I hold vintage is that it is supposed to fail slowly, and paying a third over recent trades is not slow.

There is a detail here that I would rather not have to write. As of this morning the vintage and legacy bucket is about 50% of the book at cost, the highest share it has ever carried, and it is at that high precisely because of yesterday. The box in question and an Ice Age Starter Deck at 207.58, bought the same evening, both landed in that bucket. So the error did not merely happen in the safe half. It enlarged the safe half. Whatever weighting discipline I thought I had, the mechanism that actually set my vintage allocation this week was a purchase I had not checked.

There is a second reason, and it is less comfortable. I already owned Fallen Empires product. A set of three booster packs, bought 25 November 2025 at 38.37, marked at 43.50. This is not a corner of the market I wandered into. I knew the set, I had transacted in it, and that familiarity is exactly what let me skip the check. Familiarity feels like diligence. It is the cheapest available substitute for it.

the structural problem, which is not only mine

Now the part that generalises, because I do not think this is a story about one careless evening.

The marks in this book come from public retail and marketplace data, and that phrase hides a distinction I was not treating as important enough. Marketplace data blends two very different things. A listing is a number one person is willing to say out loud. A sale is a number two people agreed on. They are not variants of the same measurement. One is an offer, the other is a fact.

On liquid product the difference does not cost you much, because listings that are wrong get corrected quickly by sales that are right. Something trades every day and the ask converges on the bid or dies. On thinly traded vintage sealed there is no such discipline. Weeks pass with nothing clearing. During those weeks the only visible numbers are asks, because posting an ask costs nothing and leaves a permanent public record, while a buyer's refusal costs nothing and leaves no record at all. Optimistic asks accumulate. Declined bids evaporate. Feed that into an aggregator and a handful of hopeful listings can set the printed market price for months.

Put another way: a screen full of asks is not a market. It is one side of a market, talking to itself, being recorded. And anyone who has marked an illiquid position from indicative quotes rather than trades knows how that ends. It works beautifully right up to the moment it matters, which is the only moment anyone ever tests it.

There is a further wrinkle that makes vintage sealed worse than the average illiquid thing, and it is worth stating because it changes how much weight the sold prices deserve. Sales here are not just rare, they are self-selecting. The boxes that clear are the ones somebody priced to clear. The ones sitting at hopeful numbers stay listed, sometimes for a very long time, and the longer they sit the more they dominate the visible sample. So the ask population and the sale population are drawn from different underlying inventories. An average of the two is not a compromise between an optimistic view and a pessimistic one. It is an average of two answers to two different questions.

The archive has been circling this for a decade without naming it. There is a January 2015 thread about being scammed 1,200 dollars on eBay, and two threads a decade apart, one in August 2017 and one this month, both about how to find sealed sellers you can trust and both warning that a deal too good to be true is exactly that, even when it comes from somewhere reputable. Those read as fraud threads. They are also pricing threads. What connects them is that a listing carries no information about the person behind it or the object in front of them, and a completed sale carries at least the fact that one buyer looked and proceeded.

Buffett put the general case in nine words a long time ago: price is what you pay, value is what you get. The version I needed on 8 August was narrower and less quotable. The number on the screen is what somebody hopes. The number in the sales history is what somebody did.

the response, which is not an argument

The tempting move here is to argue with the comp. I could tell you that completed sales in a thin market are noisy, that condition varies, that a sample of a few sales is not a market, that the box I bought may be better than the boxes that sold. Some of that would even be true. All of it would be beside the point, because I did not know any of it at the time of purchase and none of it is why I paid 534.99. Arguing with the comp is the single most expensive habit available to anyone who marks their own book. It converts a small admitted loss into a large defended one.

So the response is a policy change rather than a rebuttal. Effective from this letter, valuation in this book moves to a sold-price basis wherever completed sales exist. Listings will be used only where no sale is observable, and where a mark rests on a listing it will be flagged as such in the ledger, so that anyone reading the portfolio page can see at a glance which of my numbers are evidence and which are inference. I do not think a fund should be able to hide that distinction from its own reader, and I certainly should not be able to hide it from myself.

This will lower the marked value of the book. I am not going to guess at how much before the work is done, because guessing at it now would be the same failure in a new costume. Every one of the 37 positions is currently carried above cost, and it would be strange if all of that survived contact with completed sales. It joins two policies already committed to in print: the Hobbit block gets reviewed against post-release prices in October, and nothing gets bought inside the thirty days before a set's release date.

Three rules, all written after the mistake that produced them. That is the honest sequence and I would rather show it than pretend I arrived with a rulebook. What I will not do again is let the ledger flatter a position I have not tested.

You can buy at any price you like. You only ever sell at one somebody else has already agreed to.

Is there a floor

Every market I have watched eventually produces the word floor. It arrives in a particular tone of voice, usually late, usually from somebody who is down money and would like to stop thinking about it. There is a floor here. It cannot go below what the contents are worth. Notice what that sentence is quietly doing. It treats the floor as if someone had installed it, the way a builder pours a slab and then walks on it. But nobody poured anything. At no point in the history of this market did any party stand up and promise a holder of sealed cardboard a level beneath which the price would not travel. The floor is not a commitment anyone made. It is an inference the holder drew, on his own behalf, at a moment when he badly needed one.

In August 2023 someone in my archive asked the question directly: do sealed draft boosters have a floor. It is a good question, better than most of what surrounds it, because it is answerable in principle. And the answer that came back, in various phrasings, was the one you would expect and the one I would have given at the time. Yes, because the cards inside are worth something. A box is a container of singles. Singles have prices. Therefore the box cannot be worth less than the expected value of what falls out of it, less the friction of selling a hundred small things one at a time.

I want to take that seriously before I take it apart, because it is not a foolish argument. It is the same argument value investors have made about liquidation value for the better part of a century, and in equities it has real teeth. If a company's assets can be sold for more than the market capitalisation, you have a genuine backstop, and the reason you have it is that the assets are yours. Nobody else can revise them.

the floor is a derivative

Here is where the analogy fails, and it fails at exactly the joint that matters.

The expected value of the cards in a box is a function of singles prices. Singles prices are a function of two things: how many copies exist, and whether the cards are legal to play in the formats people actually play. The first is set by reprints. The second is set by bans and format governance. Both are decided by one company, at meetings, on a schedule of its own choosing, and neither decision requires consulting anyone who owns a sealed box.

So the floor is not a floor. It is a derivative, and the underlying is publisher policy. That is a strange thing to lean on. A floor that moves is not a floor. It is a price, and prices move, and this one moves in response to an actor whose incentives run the other way from yours. Say it plainly and it sounds obvious. It did not sound obvious to me for a long time, and I suspect it does not sound obvious to most people holding modern sealed product today.

The archive has the demonstration, and it is not a model, it is a record. In March 2017, after a Masters reprint, there is a thread observing that a set had become one of the lowest value sets to open. Read that slowly. Nothing about the boxes changed. The cardboard did not degrade. The demand for the game did not fall. Somebody printed more of the good cards, the expected value of opening the old boxes collapsed, and the supposed floor fell out from under a product that had not moved. That is the floor descending in real time, in public, with a date attached.

It is not an isolated case. In August 2014 there is a thread tracking dealer prices for the Onslaught fetchlands before and after the Khans of Tarkir reprint announcement. Those cards had gone untouched since 2002. Twelve years of restraint is a long time, long enough that a holder could be forgiven for treating it as policy. It was not policy. It was a gap. And in October 2015 there is the cleanest experiment this market has ever run without meaning to: every Khans fetchland at an all-time high except the single one reprinted again in a supplemental product. Five cards, one set, same rarity, same function in a deck. The four left alone made new highs. The reprinted one lagged. If you wanted a controlled study of what determines the value of the contents of a box, you will not find a better one.

My archive carries theme tags, and the largest is Reprint Risk & Supply Shocks, at 87 threads out of 316. That is not because the community is paranoid. It is because the community has been paying attention for fifteen years.

what a real floor looks like

Now let me argue the other side, because there is one, and it is the reason a large part of this book sits where it sits.

A floor becomes real when the value of the object stops routing through singles prices. Not when it routes through them more favourably. When it stops. That happens when a sealed package is old enough that the package itself is the collectible, and what is inside it is almost beside the point.

This book holds an Antiquities Booster Pack at 535.00 and a Revised Edition Booster Pack at 300.66, both bought on 13 May 2026, both marked up 5.0%. Nobody pays 535 dollars for one booster pack because of a calculation about the expected value of the cards inside it as playing pieces. That calculation, honestly done, would not get you close. What is being bought is an unopened object from the game's first years, surviving in a condition that becomes less common every time somebody decides to find out what is in one. The price is not a claim about contents. It is a claim about the count of surviving sealed packages, and the company that prints cards cannot print more of those.

Is that a floor? Not in the sense the 2023 thread was asking about. Collector demand can leave too, and if it leaves, these prices go with it. What it is instead is a different failure mode, and the difference is worth more than it sounds. Modern sealed can be devalued by a decision, taken in an afternoon, by people who owe you nothing. Vintage sealed can only be devalued by an audience losing interest, which is slow, visible, and reversible. One risk has a single point of failure with a calendar. The other does not.

retiring the word

I would like to stop using floor altogether, and replace it with a question that has an answer: who would have to change their mind for this to be worth very little, and are they in a position to do it?

For a modern draft booster the answer is short. One company, at one meeting, with one reprint. For a sealed pack from the early years, the answer is that essentially everyone would have to change their mind at once and stay changed. Those are not the same sentence with different nouns. They are different assets wearing the same shrink wrap.

Roughly 43% of this book sits in the vintage and legacy bucket, 19 units, 2,551.63 of cost. People sometimes assume that allocation reflects a view that old cardboard goes up faster. It does not. Over this book's short life it has not been the strongest bucket, and the strongest position I own is a set of six sleeved packs bought at Best Buy for 51.30. The vintage weight is not there because it rises differently. It is there because it fails differently, and I would rather own the risk I can name.

The 2023 thread deserved a better answer than it got, including from me. Here is mine. A floor nobody promised is just a price you have not been shown yet.

Nine years of reprints in three

A person's search history is a more honest document than anything he says about himself. It records what he actually wanted to know at two in the morning, unedited, with no audience in mind. I have come to think a forum archive works the same way, provided you stop reading the threads and start counting them.

I finished tagging mine this month. 316 threads, 2011 to 2026, pulled from four public communities: a general community for the game, a card-finance community, a market-watch community, and a competitive-play community. I gave every thread a theme, expecting the tags to be a filing convenience. They turned out to be the finding.

Reprint Risk and Supply Shocks: 87 threads. That is the largest tag in the archive, ahead of Commander demand at 83, distributor allocations at 73, retail dumps at 53, flash sales and cycle bottoms at 53, licensed crossovers at 44, bans at 41, buyouts at 33, and vintage scarcity at 32. More than one thread in four, across fifteen years and four separate communities that do not especially like each other, is at bottom about the same fear.

Ask a participant in this market what he worries about and he will tell you about demand. Whether the game is growing, whether the crossovers bring new players or drive old ones away, whether the format he plays is healthy. Count what he actually writes about at two in the morning and it is supply. He is not afraid of being unwanted. He is afraid of being diluted.

the 2023 argument

There is a thread from August 2023 making a specific and, I think, underappreciated claim: that the game had received more than nine years' worth of reprints inside a three-year window. Set aside whether the arithmetic survives a careful audit. I do not have the print data to check it and neither did the person who wrote it, since nobody outside the publisher has ever had that data. What matters is the shape of the assertion. Somebody sat down and tried to express supply growth as a rate rather than as an event.

That is a considerable intellectual step and almost nobody else in the archive takes it. Everywhere else, reprints are discussed as things that happen to a specific card on a specific day. There is an August 2014 thread tracking dealer prices for the Onslaught fetchlands before and after the Khans of Tarkir reprint announcement, cards that had been untouched since 2002 and were suddenly not. There is a February 2016 thread on an Eternal Masters confirmation. There is a March 2017 thread observing that after a Masters set lands, the reprinted set becomes one of the least rewarding things you can open. Each of those is a correct observation about one event. Together they are a description of a policy, and the 2023 thread is the only one in my collection that tries to say so.

My favourite thing in the whole archive sits between those two modes. October 2015: every Khans fetchland at an all-time high, except one. Five cards from the same set, same rarity, same function, essentially interchangeable in play. Four of them made new highs. The fifth, Windswept Heath, had been reprinted again in a supplemental product, and it lagged. That is as clean an experiment as this market will ever hand you. Everything held constant except supply, and supply was the whole answer.

reprint velocity is monetary policy

Here is the frame I have been circling for a month and have finally decided to write down.

The publisher is a central bank and reprint velocity is its policy rate. It controls the supply of a monetary-like asset. It can loosen, by printing more, into more formats, at more price points, more often. It can tighten, by holding back, by letting a set stay out of print, by honouring an old commitment. It does not announce a target, it does not publish minutes, and it is not accountable to the holders of the asset. The holders bear the entire adjustment.

I like this analogy because of how badly it fails in one specific place, and that failure is the most useful part. A central bank at least claims to have the holder's welfare somewhere in its objective function. Price stability is a promise to the person holding the currency. The publisher has made no such promise and should not be expected to make one. It has a promise to its shareholders, and the second quarter of 2026, reported on 21 July, is what keeping that promise looks like: Magic revenue up 32% to 545.3 million dollars, the first quarter above half a billion, the first half of the segment up 27% to 1.246 billion, guidance raised. Loose policy, working exactly as intended, for the party it was intended to work for.

Once you hold the frame, the archive reads differently. The September 2021 thread asking whether the publisher was cutting Draft Booster Box production is not a thread about draft boxes. It is a market attempting, without data, to guess at a change in the policy stance, from the equivalent of a rumour on a trading floor. Some threads read it as tightening and turn bullish. Some read it as a category being retired and turn bearish. Nobody can check. That is a market watching supply constantly, obsessively even, and understanding it poorly, which is the exact condition in which prices move on sentiment and then reverse.

Fifteen years of it. 87 threads. Not one of them with access to a print figure.

what you can do about it, which is less than you would like

In a normal market you would hedge this. You would find an instrument that pays when supply loosens and lose a little every month for the privilege. There is no such instrument here. There is no short. You cannot borrow a booster box and sell it. There is no options market on the publisher's print schedule, and even the publisher's own equity is a poor proxy, since the thing that hurts you is the thing that helps the shares.

So the hedge does not exist, and what remains is selection. You do not hedge a printing press. You choose things it cannot reach.

That is the honest reason the largest single block in this book sits in vintage and legacy sealed, in material printed in the 1990s. It is not nostalgia and it is not connoisseurship, since I have no particular affection for those sets. It is that the print run closed before the current audience was born and the Reserved List, a published promise honoured for three decades, prevents the most damaging kind of reissue for a defined set of cards. Whatever you think of that policy, and there are serious people who think it is bad for the game, it is the only place in this market where scarcity is a fact rather than a forecast. Those positions are dull. Dull and quietly up is a fine outcome for the part of the book whose job is to still be there.

The rest of the book is exposed, and I am not going to dress that up. The Hobbit block alone is 1,405.83 dollars at cost across 6 units, about 23.9% of a 5,873.40 cost basis, in a set released this month by the same publisher posting record revenue by making more things. My protection there is a licence, which constrains reissue without preventing it, and I have already written about how little that was worth to anyone who bought Tolkien product in 2023 believing it would be the only one.

The market has been telling on itself for fifteen years in a document nobody thought to count. Eighty-seven threads is not a discussion. It is a confession.

Who is the marginal buyer

At dinner last week I made the mistake of describing this book to someone who works in a real market. He was polite about it. He asked the usual things, what it costs, where it is stored, whether the boxes have to stay sealed, and I had good answers, because those are the questions I have practised. Then he put his fork down and asked one I had not practised.

"Who do you sell it to?"

I said something about marketplaces and completed sales and thin bids on vintage. It was true and it was not an answer, and both of us knew it inside about four seconds. He was not asking about venue. He was asking about the person. Somewhere out there is a human being who will one day own my Marvel Super Heroes Collector Booster Display, and I could not describe him. I could not tell you what he does on a Saturday, whether he plays the game, or what he intends to do with a box of cards he paid me for. I had built a position of 5,598.45 dollars at cost across 32 positions and 40 sealed units without ever writing down a description of the counterparty on the other end of the exit.

That is an embarrassing thing to admit in print, so I am admitting it in print, which is the only discipline I have that works.

the archive asks the same question, repeatedly, and does not answer it either

Here is what makes me feel slightly better and considerably worse. The question is not original to my dinner. It is one of the recurring anxieties of the market, and it turns up in the archive with a regularity that borders on comic.

June 2021: a thread asking whether Collector Booster boxes are actually valuable to players. Note the word actually, doing a lot of work. July 2025: a thread on Collector Boosters and the arrival of a new kind of large buyer, and, in the same month, a separate thread asking whether sealed collecting is dead. September 2025: a thread asking, plainly, who is actually buying Collector Booster boxes. Five years of the same question, phrased more nervously each time.

What strikes me is that nobody in these threads ever produces the buyer. They produce theories of the buyer. He is a whale. He is a content creator opening product on camera. He is a store stocking a case. He is somebody in another country where the release did not arrive. He is, in the versions written after a bad month, a person who no longer exists. The buyer is always inferred from the price rather than the price from the buyer, which is the direction of reasoning that gets people killed in every market I have ever worked in.

There is a February 2023 thread asking how many people are trapped holding sealed collector product. Trapped is the word in the thread and it is the correct word. You are not trapped when the price is low. You are trapped when there is no bid at any price you are willing to say out loud, which is a different condition and a much worse one.

the difference between a consumer and a holder

Let me put the structure plainly, because it took me two years and several thousand dollars to see it.

Some products have a natural holder at the bottom. A Play Booster box is bought, in the end, by a person who wants to open it and play with what falls out. That person is not price-insensitive, but he is real, and he has a reservation price which is a function of entertainment rather than resale. When speculative demand leaves a product like that, the price falls to where the player is, and the player is a floor. Not a high floor. A floor.

Some products do not have that person. A Collector Booster is a premium object, priced well above what a player pays for the same cards in another wrapper, and its purchase is very often justified by what it will be worth rather than by what it contains. When speculative demand leaves that product, there is nothing underneath it, because nobody was ever buying it for the experience at that price. The bid does not fall to a floor. It falls to wherever the next speculator is standing, and if he has also stepped back, the search continues downward with no obvious stopping point.

This is not theory. The presale record from this year is a controlled experiment. Collector Booster boxes for the Spider-Man and the Avatar crossovers were bid toward 1,000 dollars ahead of release, and then settled back to roughly 750 and 525 respectively once the product actually reached shelves. Nothing changed about the cards. What changed is that the people who were buying to sell ran out of people to sell to, and the market went looking for whoever was left. Final Fantasy did the opposite and recovered strongly, and the reason matters: it had a chase card that stayed genuinely scarce inside sealed product. In other words, it had a reason to be opened, which is another way of saying it had a consumer.

A product bought mainly by people who intend to resell it has no natural holder at the bottom. Price discovery in that product is entirely a question of who is left when the resellers stop.

now apply it to me

I hold a Marvel Super Heroes Collector Booster Display bought on 3 June at 502.33, marked at 526.70. I hold a Marvel Super Heroes Collector Booster Box bought on 6 June at 534.95, marked at 559.98. And I hold the Hobbit Collector Booster Display bought on 18 July at 839.85, marked at 859.28, which is the largest position in the book.

That is 1,877.13 dollars at cost in the exact product category the archive has spent five years failing to identify a buyer for. It is a third of my invested capital. All three are marked above cost today, and I want to be honest about what that marking is worth. My marks between observations are straight-line modelled, and a recent purchase therefore drifts upward on a schedule rather than on evidence. A mark is not a bid. On thin sealed product the gap between a listed price and a completed sale can be a third of the value, and I take marks from listings as well as from sales, which means my sheet is flattered by exactly the ambiguity this letter is about.

Set against that, I hold a Lord of the Rings Set Booster Box bought in May at 337.10, a Hobbit Bundle at 74.89, and, from June 2024, six sleeved Lord of the Rings packs bought off a shelf at Best Buy for 51.30 and marked today at 84.36. Those are the least glamorous line items on the sheet. The 51.30 is also, by some distance, the best performing thing I own, up 64.4%, which I have decided not to read as a coincidence. If the speculative bid disappears entirely from all of them, I know who wants the contents, and I can picture him.

I am not going to draw a recommendation out of this, because this is a personal book and I do not give advice. I will draw a question, and it is the one my friend handed me across the table without knowing he was giving me a research agenda. Before the price, before the print run, before the chase card, before the licence: name the person. Not the category, not the segment. The person. If you cannot describe him well enough to picture him putting his card details into a website at eleven at night, you do not know what you own.

Every market has a buyer of last resort. Mine is a man who wants to open the box, and for two years I have been outbidding him.

Letter 008 · 27 July 2026 · Miami, Florida

Printed to demand

Start with a thought experiment, because I would rather you arrive at the conclusion before I state it. Pick any collectible you like. Coins, stamps, first editions, a run of numbered prints from an artist whose work you would actually hang. Now change one rule and leave everything else alone. The issuer gets to see the order book before deciding how many to make. Orders open on a Friday, they close three weeks later, and the issuer then produces exactly the quantity ordered. No more, no fewer. Everyone who wanted one gets one. Nobody who wanted one is turned away.

What is that object worth? Not what does it cost on the Friday, which is set by the issuer and tells you nothing. What is it worth in five years, when the people who bought it want to know what they have. Sit with it for a second, because the answer is not zero, and it is not obvious, and it is almost certainly not the number the people who queued on the Friday have in their heads.

Notice also that nobody in this arrangement has behaved badly. The issuer is being responsive to its customers, which is what we say we want from companies. The buyer got the thing he asked for at the price he was quoted. There is no shortage, no queue, no reseller marking it up on the doorstep. It is, on every measure a consumer would use, a better experience than the old way. And it has quietly removed the one property that made collectibles behave like assets in the first place: an unmanaged relationship between how many exist and how many are wanted.

There is a thread in my archive from September 2020 arguing that a particular direct-to-consumer crossover product was bad for the long-term financial health of the game. I have read it twice now. The complaint on the surface is aesthetic, and it is easy to dismiss on those grounds, the way you dismiss anyone who says a thing was better before. The argument underneath is not aesthetic at all, and I think the person making it may not have fully seen what he had. He was not really objecting to the artwork. He was objecting to a mechanism.

two kinds of scarcity

I keep coming back to a distinction that I now think is the whole game.

A closed print run is a fact. Some number of units was made, the plates came down, and whatever happens to demand afterwards, that number cannot go up. You may not know what it is. In this market you usually do not, because the publisher has never disclosed print runs and has no reason to start. But your ignorance of the number does not make the number soft. It exists, it is finite, and every box that gets opened moves it in one direction only.

A closed ordering window is a schedule. It is a date on somebody else's calendar. It produces the same feeling as scarcity, the same urgency, the same fear of missing, and it produces none of the substance. When the window shuts, the quantity is not fixed by anything except how many people happened to want one during three weeks in a particular autumn. If that number was large, the print run is large. The scarcity you paid for was the scarcity of the deadline, and deadlines are free to manufacture.

I want to be fair about this, because print-to-demand is a genuinely elegant piece of business. Consider what it does to the publisher's working capital. No unsold inventory. No returns. No guessing. No writing down a warehouse of a set nobody liked. Compare that against the older arrangement, where product went out through distributors on allocations that nobody outside the system could see and everybody inside the system complained about. Seventy-three threads in my archive are tagged for distributor allocations and wholesale supply, which is a lot of ink spilled on the question of who got how much and why. Print-to-demand answers that question by making it irrelevant. Everyone got as much as they asked for. That is the feature. That is also the problem.

what the publisher found out

The larger discovery, and I think it is the single most important structural change of the last decade, is that the publisher can sell directly. Once you can reach the buyer yourself, you learn two things at once: what he will pay, and how many of him there are. Before, you shipped into a channel and read the results months later through a fog of allocations and store-level pricing. There is a thread from January 2014 patiently explaining to somebody why shops charge more than the suggested retail price. That thread is a fossil of an era when the publisher genuinely did not control the last mile.

Now it does, at least for the part of the range it chooses to sell that way. And the numbers say the strategy is working. Magic revenue reached 1.72 billion dollars in 2025, up roughly 59% year over year. The second quarter of 2026, reported on 21 July, put Magic revenue at 545.3 million, up 32%, the first quarter above half a billion in the history of the game. Full-year guidance was raised. If you hold the shares, this is a very good story. If you hold the cardboard, you should read the same sentences and feel something closer to vertigo, because that revenue was produced by making and selling more things, and you are long the scarcity of things.

One number in that release is worth more than the headline. The operating margin for the segment came in at 40.7%, against an adjusted 46.3% a year earlier, and the compression was attributed to product development and marketing. That is a company spending money to build more product lines. Whatever else you conclude, do not conclude that the pace slows from here.

formats are levers

Watch the product formats, because they are the tell. In February 2020 the archive picks up the announcement of Jumpstart, a new format. By July 2020 there is a thread on the introduction of Set Boosters alongside Zendikar Rising. Each arrival was presented as a better way to experience the game, and each was probably sincere in that. Each was also a new lever. A format is a way to sell the same cards at a different price to a different person, and once you have four of them you can raise supply without ever announcing that you have raised supply.

Then December 2024, and a thread full of people angry that Set Boosters had been ruined. I have read the complaints and I do not particularly care about the contents of the argument. I care that it happened at all. A format that was introduced in 2020 as a solution had, within four years, been changed enough that the people who liked it felt robbed. That is the correct way to understand every product line in this market. Not as objects with stable definitions, but as settings on a machine, adjustable at will by a party that is not consulting you and does not owe you notice.

This year makes the point without needing my commentary. 2026 carries seven sets, four of them licensed crossovers and three of them original worlds, the first year in which the borrowed properties outnumber the game's own. The head designer has called it an outlier and signalled a return to a three-and-three split. Perhaps. I would observe that outliers announced after the fact are usually experiments, and that experiments which raise revenue by 32% are rarely abandoned.

where this sits in the book

I will not pretend to be watching this from outside. My largest single position is a Hobbit Collector Booster Display bought on 18 July at 839.85. Wizards priced the Collector Booster at 37.99, which makes twelve of them 455.88 at retail. I paid roughly eighty percent over the cost of the cardboard for a product whose supply is set by how many people ordered one, in a window that has not been described to me, and which I bought in the stretch between the end of preview season and the release date, which the record says is the worst window there is.

I bought it anyway. The thesis was the chase card, a Gleaming Gold Smaug at roughly 500 copies appearing in under 1% of Collector Boosters, and I still think that is the right variable to watch. But notice the shape of what I did. I bought a bet on a numerator, 500 copies, inside a denominator that nobody has told me and that was determined after my order was placed. That is not scarcity. That is a lottery with an undisclosed number of tickets sold.

The first purchase this book ever made was the opposite kind of thing. Six sleeved Lord of the Rings packs off a shelf at Best Buy in June 2024, 51.30 for the lot, marked today at 84.36 and still the best performing line on the sheet. I paid the shelf price, which is as close to the cost of production as a public buyer ever gets, and I did it without a window, a preview season or a countdown. Two years later it is up 64.4% and the display I agonised over is up 2.3%. I do not think that is skill. I think it is what happens when you buy something after the machine has finished deciding how many to make, rather than while it is still asking.

A closed print run tells you how many exist. A closed window only tells you when they stopped asking.

A history lesson from the fetchlands

Every serious market eventually gets studied. Somebody notices that a policy change hit one group of assets and not another, that the two groups were otherwise alike, and that the difference between them is therefore measurable. They write it up. Other people argue with the write-up. Twenty years later there is a body of knowledge, imperfect and contested, but there.

This market runs experiments of that quality constantly, several times a year, and keeps no notes at all. The results get argued about for a fortnight on a forum, the thread falls off the front page, and the next time the same thing happens everyone starts from scratch with the same opinions they had before. I have 316 of those threads in a folder now, and the reason I assembled them is that I think the folder is the only lab notebook this market has.

So let me use it. Here is the cleanest experiment in the whole archive, and it is more than a decade old.

In September 2014, Khans of Tarkir reprinted the Onslaught fetchlands. These were format staples that had not been printed since 2002. Twelve years of supply, fixed, against twelve years of a growing player base. Then a single announcement, and the supply schedule for five specific cards changed.

lesson one: price moves on the announcement

There is a thread in the archive from 31 August 2014 that tracks dealer prices for those cards before and after the reprint was announced. It is not sophisticated. It is a person writing down numbers, which is exactly what nobody does.

What it shows is that the repricing happened on the announcement. Not on the print run, not on the shipment, not on the day the new cards appeared in shops. On the announcement. At the moment the price moved, precisely zero additional cards existed in the world. The physical supply of Onslaught fetchlands was, on the day after the news, identical to the supply on the day before.

That should be obvious to anyone who has watched any market. It is not obvious in this one, where a very large fraction of the discussion is people waiting to see how much product turns up before deciding what something is worth. If you are waiting for the supply, you are waiting for a fact that the price consumed months ago. Reprint risk prices on information. It does not price on cardboard.

lesson two: five cards, one difference

Now the good part, and the reason I called this an experiment rather than an anecdote.

A thread from October 2015 observes that every Khans of Tarkir fetchland had reached an all-time high. Every one except a single card, Windswept Heath, which had been reprinted a second time in a supplemental product, the Origins Clash Pack.

Stop and consider what you have there. Five cards from the same set. Same rarity. Same printing. Same function in the same formats. Same audience, same shelf, same year. Every variable you would want to control for is controlled for by construction, and one treatment was applied to one of the five. Four made new highs. The one that got printed again lagged.

You will not get a cleaner read than that in any market anywhere. It cost nothing to observe. It required no model. And it tells you the thing that actually matters, which is that this market does price supply, properly and persistently, when the supply difference is real and everything else is held constant. The scepticism you sometimes hear, that sealed and singles prices are pure sentiment and reprints are a scare story, does not survive those five cards.

lesson three: the bears were right for a month

And now the part that complicates it, which is why I did not stop two paragraphs ago.

The same cards that were marked down on the September 2014 announcement were making new all-time highs a little over a year later. Both things are in the archive. The people who said the reprint would hurt the price were right, and they were right almost immediately. The people who said the cards would be worth more than ever were also right, and they were right on a longer clock.

The resolution is that a reprint is a supply event inside a demand trend. If demand is growing underneath, additional supply resets the level and the trend reasserts itself. If demand is flat or falling, the reset is the whole story and there is nothing underneath to reassert.

The archive supplies the counter-case too, and I would be cherry-picking if I left it out. A thread from June 2015 has Modern Masters 2015 staples drifting back toward their opening weekend lows. A thread from March 2017 argues that after a Masters reprint a set becomes one of the least rewarding things you can open. Another from August 2023 claims the game absorbed more than nine years' worth of reprints inside three years. Those are not the fetchland story. Those are what the fetchland story looks like when the trend underneath is not there, or when the reprints arrive faster than the audience grows.

Which is the honest summary of the whole lesson: reprint risk is not a question about supply. It is a question about whether supply is arriving faster than demand, and the answer is different for every set, in every year, and it is never available on the day of the announcement, which is exactly when everybody demands it.

what this costs my book

Apply it here, since that is the point of keeping notes.

The vintage sleeve of this book cannot be charged this tax. The Antiquities pack, the Urza's Saga material, the Revised booster, the Fallen Empires packs, the 1,952 dollars I deployed across six positions on 13 May: those print runs closed in the last century and the Reserved List forecloses the announcement that would reprice them. There is no thread to be written about the day the supply schedule for a booster pack printed in the 1990s changed, because there will not be such a day. That is not a prediction. It is the absence of a mechanism.

The crossover sleeve can be charged this tax every single year, and it has been. Look at what happened to the 2023 Tolkien set the moment a second Tolkien set was announced. There is a thread from May 2026 working through what the Hobbit reveals mean for existing Lord of the Rings cards. That is the fetchland announcement all over again, in a different costume, assessed on holders of a set nobody thought could be followed. I hold that set. The 337.10 Set Booster Box from May, and a handful of smaller Tolkien positions going back to the six sleeved packs that were the first thing I ever bought, in June 2024, at 51.30. Those have been good to me. They were also, without my noticing at the time, an unhedged bet that a licensor with a profitable partnership would decline to renew it.

None of this means the crossover sleeve should not exist in the book. It means the two sleeves are exposed to different things and should never be sized as though they are the same thing. A licence is a delay. A published promise is a floor. I am paying for the difference either way, and I would rather know which one I am buying.

Four of those five cards made new highs and one did not, and the only difference between them was a decision taken in an office. That is not market risk. That is counterparty risk with better artwork.

I bought the top and I knew it

Yesterday afternoon, a Saturday, I clicked buy on a Hobbit Collector Booster Display at 839.85 dollars. It is now the largest position in this book by more than three hundred dollars, ahead of a sealed Antiquities booster pack that I had thought of as the anchor of the whole enterprise.

The page had not finished reloading and I already knew what I had done. That is the part worth writing down, because a mistake you make in ignorance is a tuition payment and a mistake you make with the arithmetic on the screen in front of you is something else entirely.

So let me put the arithmetic on the screen for you too.

The publisher priced the Collector Booster for this set at 37.99. There are twelve of them in a box. Twelve times 37.99 is 455.88. That is what the cardboard costs at the counter. I paid 839.85. I paid roughly eighty percent over retail for a product that has not been released, which means the eighty percent is not compensation for scarcity that has been demonstrated. It is a fee for conviction about scarcity that has been asserted.

And I paid it in the window between the end of preview season and the release date, which is historically the worst window in this market to be a buyer. It is the point of maximum imagination and minimum information. Every card has been revealed, so the excitement is fully priced. No product has shipped, so nobody yet knows how much of it there will be. If you were designing a moment to separate an enthusiast from his money, you would design that one.

what I actually bought

I have gone back through my own reasoning from Friday and Saturday, and I want to be precise about what I find, because vagueness here would be a kindness to myself that I have not earned.

Every input I used was true. The licence constrains the publisher in a way its own worlds do not. Tolkien product has held up better in this book than anything else of comparable vintage. Collector Boosters are the only sleeve that carries the highest-end treatments. There is a candidate chase card. All true, all defensible, all things I would say again in public.

Let me steelman myself for a paragraph, because I do not want this letter to be theatre either, and there is a real argument on the other side. Retail price is not a clearing price. A box at 37.99 a pack assumes you can get twelve packs at 37.99 a pack, which for a Collector Booster case is rarely true for anyone who is not standing in the right shop on the right morning. Allocation is real. The premium over retail in this product category is not a pure sentiment tax, it is partly the price of not having to hunt.

I believe that argument. I also notice that it is an argument about paying some premium and I used it to justify paying eighty percent. That is how these things work. The good reason is genuine and it is doing the work of covering an amount it was never sized to cover. If the honest allocation premium is meaningful and the honest sentiment premium is the rest, then I have paid a defensible price for one part of the position and an indefensible one for the other, and I do not get to average the two and call the whole thing reasonable.

Those threads describe a product whose bid is narrow and whose holding base is a thin one. A February 2023 thread asks how many people are trapped holding sealed collector product. I own the archive. I am the one who assembled it. And I bought into the most crowded window of the most crowded product category in it.

The Hobbit has a candidate chase card, a Gleaming Gold Smaug, roughly 500 copies in existence, appearing in under one percent of Collector Boosters. On its face that is the Final Fantasy setup: a card scarce enough that opening product to chase it is a losing proposition for most people, which supports the sealed price rather than draining it.

Is 500 copies scarce enough to hold up a box price eighty percent over retail? I do not know. I want to write that plainly rather than dressing it in a range. "I do not know" is a perfectly respectable input. It is not an acceptable input to the largest position in a book. But a mark that flatters me is worse than useless, because it is the mechanism by which a bad position becomes a permanent one.

Howard Marks has a line I keep coming back to: experience is what you got when you didn't get what you wanted. I have not lost money on this position. It is too early to know anything of the sort. What I have is the experience, delivered in advance and at full price, and it turns out you can buy that at retail plus eighty percent.

I did not buy a supply-constrained asset yesterday. I bought a Tolkien book with a spreadsheet stapled to the front, and I wrote the spreadsheet myself.

Letter 005 · 15 July 2026 · Miami, Florida

Reading the issuer's accounts

I spent an evening last week doing something dull. I went through the theme tags on the fund's archive and counted. Eighty-seven threads tagged reprint risk and supply shocks. Seventy-three tagged distributor allocations and wholesale supply. Fifty-three tagged retail dumps. Fifty-three more on flash sales and cycle bottoms. Set aside the overlap and you are still looking at the better part of two hundred conversations, spread across fifteen years, whose entire subject is how much product exists and who is holding it back.

Then I went looking for the threads that answered the question by reading the company's accounts. I could not find one. Not a bad one, not a wrong one. I could not find a discussion in the archive that was built on the issuer's own audited numbers.

Think about how strange that is. This is a market that talks about supply constantly, to the point of tedium, with real money at stake and real anger in the posts. The supply is set by a single publicly listed company. That company files quarterly. The filings are free, they are audited, they are indexed, they are searchable, and they are the only document in this entire market that somebody has signed their name to under legal penalty. Everything else is a rumour about a pallet.

I have a theory about why. People here think of the company as the maker of a game they love or resent, which is a relationship, not an analysis. Almost nobody thinks of it as the issuer of the thing they own. But that is exactly what it is. If you hold sealed product, you hold an asset whose supply is decided by a public company with a quarterly revenue target and an investor call to get through.

the income statement is a supply schedule

Here is the sentence I want to put at the centre of this letter, and I am aware it will sound backwards the first time you read it.

Record revenue is record supply.

In 2025, Magic revenue reached 1.72 billion dollars, up roughly 59% on the prior year, carried by the Final Fantasy crossover. Every write-up I saw treated that as a demand number. It is a demand number. It is also, and more usefully to anyone holding sealed boxes, a receipt. Revenue is what the company collected for cardboard that was printed, packed, shipped and paid for. A 59% increase in what was collected is not a signal that the market is hungry. It is a measurement of how much got made.

Now let me argue with myself, because the point is softer than I just made it sound. Revenue is price times units, and the mix in this market has been shifting toward more expensive packaging for years. Collector Boosters carry a higher price than the product they replaced. So revenue can rise faster than units, and some of that 59% is people paying more for the same number of cards rather than more cards existing. That is a real objection and I do not want to wave it away.

It does not rescue the position, though. It only tells you the number overstates the unit growth by some amount you cannot measure from outside. Direction is still direction. A company that collected 1.72 billion dollars in a year printed more than a company that collected considerably less, and the sealed float that existed at the start of that year was competing with all of it by the end.

what the company says out loud

The filings are the audited part. The commentary around them is unaudited and, for my purposes, at least as informative, because it tells you what the organisation is being pointed at.

The framing I keep returning to is the company's own comparison of its two biggest crossovers. The Lord of the Rings set took about six months to generate 200 million dollars of revenue. Final Fantasy did the same in a single day. That was offered to investors as a triumph, and it was one. Read it from where I sit, holding sealed product, and it is something else. It is a public statement, by management, about which kind of release earns applause. Nobody inside that building needed a memo after that sentence was said out loud.

Then there is the calendar, which is the part I find most useful and which is not hidden anywhere. 2026 carries seven sets. Four are crossovers, three are Magic's own worlds. It is the first year in the game's history in which borrowed universes outnumber original ones. Lorwyn Eclipsed in January, Teenage Mutant Ninja Turtles in March, Secrets of Strixhaven in April, Marvel Super Heroes in June, The Hobbit in August, with Reality Fracture and Star Trek still ahead of us. That list was public well before most of those sets were released. It is not a rumour and it is not a leak. It is a supply schedule, published a year in advance, and anyone buying sealed product in this market had it available for free.

The company's head designer has called 2026 an outlier and signalled a return to three in-universe sets and three crossovers. I believe he means it. I also note that this is guidance, not a covenant. There is a difference between a company saying what it intends and a company binding itself, and the whole of my confidence in the older end of my book rests on that difference. The Reserved List is a promise not to earn revenue from certain cards, kept for three decades. It appears nowhere on the income statement, because a promise not to sell something never does. That absence is the most valuable line item the company has.

what I will do next Tuesday

The second quarter numbers arrive on Tuesday. I am not going to trade them, because I do not trade, and because a quarterly print is far too fast a clock for an asset I intend to hold for years. I am going to read them the way I would read the accounts of a company whose paper I own, which is what they are.

What I want from the document is not the growth rate. It is whether the growth is coming from more product or dearer product, what the margin does while that happens, and what the company says about the second half of a year that still has two sets in it. If a company is spending more to sell more, its incentive to keep selling more does not weaken. It hardens.

I say all this as someone with a foot on both sides. On 13 May I deployed roughly 1,952 dollars across six positions in material printed in the 1990s, where the print run closed before most of the current audience was born and no filing on earth can change the number of boxes that exist. In June I put 502.33 and 534.95 into two Marvel Super Heroes Collector Booster cases, product from a set printed this year by a company that has told its investors, in plain language, that it intends to print more. Both purchases were deliberate. Only one of them is a fact rather than a forecast, and I would rather write that down now than discover it later.

This market spends its evenings guessing at the print run. The company publishes it four times a year and calls it revenue.

Letter 004 · 11 July 2026 · Miami, Florida

Junk wax

Somewhere there is a garage with cardboard boxes stacked against the back wall, and inside them, in plastic sheets and rubber bands, several thousand baseball cards printed between the middle 1980s and the early 1990s. Somebody's father bought them. He bought them carefully. He kept the corners sharp, he did not write on the backs, he bought complete factory sets and left the boxes shut. He had read that a card from the 1950s was worth more than his car, and he had heard the story everyone of his generation had heard, about a mother who threw the shoebox out.

He was not a fool. He was early to a correct general idea and late to a specific one. The general idea, that printed cardboard tied to a mass audience can hold value across decades, was right and remains right. The specific idea, that the cards he was buying would become rare, was wrong for a reason he had no way to check from his garage. The manufacturers had noticed the demand and were printing into it, in quantities nobody outside the companies could see. And every other careful father in the country was doing precisely what he was doing, for precisely the same reason, having read precisely the same magazine.

That is the junk wax era in a paragraph, and there is a thread in my archive from December 2025 titled exactly that, applying the whole analogy to sealed Magic. It is the most uncomfortable thread in the collection. I want to work through it in public, slowly, because a large part of this book is standing on the wrong side of it.

the half that is right

Record revenue is record supply. There is no way around that sentence and I have tried to find one.

Magic revenue reached 1.72 billion dollars in 2025, up roughly 59% year over year, carried by a single crossover. That is a remarkable commercial result and it is also, read as a supply figure, an enormous quantity of manufactured cardboard leaving factories and entering the world. You cannot book 1.72 billion dollars by selling scarcity. You book it by selling volume.

Then look at the release calendar rather than the revenue line. 2026 carries seven sets, four of them licensed crossovers and three of them original Magic worlds, the first year in the game's history in which the borrowed universes outnumber the native one. The most telling detail is not mine and not a critic's. The publisher's own head of design called 2026 an outlier and signalled a return to three and three. When the person responsible for the calendar looks at the calendar and says it is too much, a holder of sealed product should read that as a supply statement, not as reassurance.

And then the second half of the junk wax mechanism, which is the half people forget. It was not just that the companies printed a lot. It was that collectors saved it. Product held in quantity by people who intend to hold it does not become scarce, no matter how long they hold it. My archive is full of people noticing this about Magic in real time: a thread from February 2023 on how many people are trapped holding sealed collector product, one from June 2021 asking whether Collector Booster boxes are actually valuable to players, one from September 2025 asking who is genuinely buying them.

The archive's own shape argues the same point. 316 threads, one from 2011, and 119 from 2026 alone. The distribution is thin through the middle years and thick at both ends. Attention is not price, but attention at this concentration is at minimum a statement that a great many people are watching the same shelf.

the half that is wrong

Two differences separate sealed Magic from that garage, and neither of them is small.

The first is consumption. Junk wax was never consumed. Nobody destroyed one of those sets by using it, because there was nothing to use it for. The cards did what they were bought to do simply by existing, which meant the population only ever went up and never went down. Magic sealed product is destroyed by the single thing its buyers most want to do with it. Every box opened leaves the sealed float and does not return. That is a supply mechanism baseball cards never had at any point in their history.

The second is the Reserved List, a published promise not to reprint a specific set of older cards, honoured for three decades. Baseball had no equivalent and could not have had one, because no single party controlled the printing of the past. That promise is why the quietest part of this book sits in material from the 1990s. It converts scarcity that depends on somebody's restraint into scarcity that is simply a fact.

I have now written two comforting paragraphs and I would like to take both of them apart.

The consumption argument protects sealed product only where there is a live reason to open it. That March 2017 thread again: after a reprint, a set becomes one of the lowest value sets to open, and boxes of it stop being consumed. The float freezes at the print run. So the mechanism that distinguishes Magic from junk wax switches off under exactly the conditions that made junk wax junk. It is a conditional defence, and the condition is demand.

The Reserved List defence is narrower still. It covers a specific set of older cards and nothing else. It does not cover one single card in any set released in the last three years. Whatever protection it provides, it provides none whatsoever to the majority of what I own.

the part I would rather not write

So let me put the book against the analogy and see what survives.

Vintage and Legacy sealed is about 47.8% of net asset value, the largest sleeve in the book by a clear distance. That is the part with a closed print run behind it and a three decade promise underneath it. Then there is everything else. The Hobbit block, which I have only started building ahead of the August release, is 565.98 at cost across 5 units, about 11.9% of the book. Marvel Super Heroes is two collector units bought in June, at 502.33 and 534.95. Tales of Middle-earth is the 2023 crossover I have held longest, accumulated in pieces since 2024. Spider-Man is one unit from February at 78.10. Add those four sleeves together and the licensed material is already the majority of this book's cost basis, not a satellite position, and the Hobbit line is the one I expect to keep adding to between now and release.

Every one of those sets was printed within the last three years, by a company reporting record revenue, into demand it announced publicly and enthusiastically, in an era when collectors are storing sealed product in quantities that nobody can measure and everybody assumes are large. Written out like that, it is a junk wax profile. I am not going to soften the sentence.

The evidence is not merely theoretical either. Collector Booster boxes for the Spider-Man and Avatar crossovers were bid toward 1,000 dollars in presale and settled back to roughly 750 and 525 once the product actually reached shelves. That is not a demand collapse. That is supply arriving. There is a thread from August 2025 noting the Avatar set showing discounts before it had even shipped. Final Fantasy did the opposite and recovered strongly, and the differentiator was not the strength of the licence, which was never in doubt. It was a chase card that stayed genuinely scarce inside sealed product.

Against all of which, one honest counterweight from my own marks. The Tales of Middle-earth bucket, the oldest crossover I hold, is up strongly and is the best performing bucket in the book. It is licensed, it was mass produced, it was saved by collectors in quantity, and three years of people opening boxes appear to have done some work anyway. So the crossover sleeve is not uniformly junk. It is untested, which is a different word and a more accurate one.

Galbraith's point about financial memory being short applies here with a twist. This market's memory is not short. It is long and it is loud and it is 316 threads deep, and it has been repeating the junk wax warning for years to an audience that keeps buying. Memory has not been the constraint. Believing the memory applies to you has been.

The father in the garage did not lose money because he bought cardboard. He lost it because he was not the only careful man in America.

Letter 003 · 7 July 2026 · Miami, Florida

The box you cannot open

In August 2013, someone posted a question that has stayed with me longer than almost anything else in this archive. He owned several Modern Masters booster boxes. He wanted to open one. He could not decide, so he asked a forum full of strangers to tell him what to do.

Sit with the shape of that for a second. The boxes were his. He had paid for them and nobody was stopping him. He was not short of information either, since he knew what the box cost and roughly what was inside. He was asking for permission, and people ask for permission when the decision in front of them is not really about money.

I think he had noticed something real without having the vocabulary for it. He had worked out that the box in his hand was not a box of cards. It was a box of cards plus something else, and the something else would not survive the act of finding out what the cards were. Opening it would exercise the option and destroy the asset in the same motion, with one thumbnail, in under a minute, permanently.

Financial markets do not offer many instruments like that. An option on a stock can be exercised and the stock is still there afterwards. A bond redeems and the issuer carries on. Here the act of collection annihilates the thing collected. He was right to hesitate. He was also, in a way he could not have known, standing at one of the tiny hinges on which the entire supply side of this market turns.

float destruction, seen from the inside

I wrote in the first of these letters that sealed product loses supply quickly and on purpose, and that the purpose belongs to the very people whose enthusiasm holds the price up. That is a view from above. The 2013 thread is the same fact seen from inside a living room.

The number of sealed boxes of any set in the world is not a figure the publisher sets. The publisher sets one term of it. The other term is the running total of thousands of private decisions like that man's, each made for reasons that have nothing to do with anybody's portfolio. Someone opens a box because a friend is coming over. Someone opens one because they got a raise, or lost a job, or moved apartments and got tired of carrying it. The float is the residue of all of that.

Which produces the reflexive property that I find the most interesting thing about this asset class and the hardest to trade around. The float contracts fastest when the bid is strongest. Excitement about a set is simultaneously the reason people want sealed boxes and the reason those boxes stop existing. Demand eats its own supply.

Now let me argue against myself, because the loop does not run away the way that description suggests. Every dollar the sealed box rises raises the cost of opening it. At some point the box is worth conspicuously more than the cards likely to come out of it, and at that point the rational owner stops being a consumer and becomes a holder. Destruction slows exactly as the price justifies it. The float, in other words, is anchored to the gap between what a box costs and what it is expected to yield when opened, and that gap is self-correcting.

Then run it the other way, which is the direction that costs money. There is a thread from March 2017 observing that after a set gets hit by a Masters reprint, it becomes one of the lowest value sets to open. When the expected contents collapse, nobody opens the boxes. So supply stops shrinking at precisely the moment demand for the contents falls away. The float freezes at whatever the print run happened to be, and it stays frozen forever. Scarcity without an audience, again. This market has a talent for offering you the wrong scarcity at the wrong time.

the rule, and its problem

This book holds 31 positions and 39 sealed units. Not one of them has ever been opened. Nothing has ever been sold either, but the sealed rule is the stricter of the two, because a sale is reversible in the sense that the same product can be bought back, and an opening is not reversible in any sense at all.

The rule is not sentiment about wrappers. It exists for two dull reasons. First, an opened box stops being comparable to itself: the position turns into a pile of singles that must be marked individually, and a book that mixes the two is a book whose returns cannot be audited by anybody, including me. Second, opening converts a distribution into a realisation, and a realisation is a far easier thing to be publicly wrong about. So the rule is 100% sealed, permanently, and it is written down where I cannot quietly amend it.

Here is the problem with all of that, and I would rather write it myself than have a reader find it. A rule that has never been tested is not yet a rule. It is a preference with good grammar.

Roughly 93% of this book's cost basis was deployed after 1 May 2026. The rule has therefore been in force, under anything resembling real temptation, for a period measured in weeks. I hold two Marvel Super Heroes collector units bought in early June, at 502.33 and 534.95, and I have not opened either. I would like to report that as discipline. Honestly, it is not, because I have not once wanted to. Refusing something you do not want is not restraint, it is arithmetic.

The genuine test is coming and I can see the date on it. The Hobbit arrives in August, and it has a candidate chase card, roughly 500 copies in existence, turning up in fewer than one in a hundred Collector Boosters. That is the configuration that breaks rules. Not a card worth a fortune, a card worth a fortune at odds bad enough that a reasonable person can talk himself into one more box. Whatever collector product I hold going into that release will be sitting there through the exact weeks when opening it is most exciting, most social, and most defensible after the fact.

Howard Marks has a line I keep coming back to: "You can't predict. You can prepare." I cannot predict how I will feel in August. Writing the rule down in July, in public, with a date on it, is the only preparation available.

what the option is really worth

I have been calling the sealed box an option, and I should be honest about where that language stops working, because borrowed vocabulary flatters an argument in ways the argument has not earned.

It resembles an option in the ways that matter most. It has an underlying whose price is publicly observable, since every card that could be inside is quoted somewhere. It has no expiry. It has a carry cost, in storage, in the shelf space, in the capital not doing something else. And it has an asymmetry that anyone who has held one recognises, which is that the good outcome is much better than the bad outcome is bad.

Where it stops resembling an option is on the other side of the ledger. In a real options market you know how many contracts are outstanding. Here nobody does. The print run is undisclosed and the destruction rate is unmeasurable, so the one quantity that would let you price the thing properly is the one quantity nobody has. Every valuation of sealed product, mine included, is an estimate of contents dressed up as an estimate of scarcity.

Which is why I keep returning to a stranger's post from thirteen years ago. He was not asking whether to open a box. He was asking someone to tell him what he owned. Nobody could, and nobody can, and that is the position.

Answers are the cheap part of this market. The expensive part is the wrapper that stops you from having one.

Letter 002 · 3 July 2026 · Miami, Florida

Singles versus the box

A sealed booster box does not weigh much. A few dozen packs of cardboard, a printed sleeve, a strip of shrink wrap that has gone slightly cloudy if the box is old enough to have sat under a shop light. You can hold one in one hand. You can read every word printed on the outside of it, the set name, the pack count, the legal type running along the bottom edge. And the single thing you actually want to know is the one thing the box will not tell you.

I did this in June with the Tales of Middle-earth Set Booster box I bought in May for 337.10. I picked it up, turned it over twice, and caught myself doing arithmetic about what was inside. Then I put it back down, which is the rule in this book, and the arithmetic stayed unfinished. That unfinished arithmetic is, I have come to think, the entire product. Not a side effect of the product. The product.

Because here is the strange part of holding a sealed box. You are not holding cards. You are holding a sealed statement about cards, and the statement is probabilistic. Somewhere in there are commons that nobody has wanted since the day they were printed. Somewhere in there, possibly, is the card that pays for the box. Both of those things are true at once, and they stay true for exactly as long as you leave the wrapper alone.

The oldest fight in this market is about this and nothing else. There is a thread in my archive from November 2013 arguing singles against sealed, and another from May 2017 doing it all over again, and a second May 2017 thread that runs the most useful version of the test: it sets the cost of a booster box beside the cost of simply buying the individual cards you wanted out of it, and asks which is the better use of the money. Both sides show up. Both sides are confident. Neither wins, then or in the nine years since, and I think the reason they never win is that they are not arguing about which asset is better. They are arguing as though they own the same asset.

They do not. A single card and a sealed box are two different instruments that happen to be made of the same material, in roughly the way a bond and the company that issued it are made of the same business.

what a single actually is

A single card is a claim on demand for one card. That is the whole of it. Its simplicity is its virtue and also its exposure, because there is nothing standing between the holder and the two things that can happen to a card: people can stop wanting it, or more of it can appear.

My archive tags 87 of its 316 threads under reprint risk and supply shocks, and 83 under Commander demand. Those are the two largest theme tags by a distance, and they are not two topics. They are the numerator and the denominator of every single card's price. Demand from a format, divided by however many copies the publisher has decided exist.

The cleanest illustration I have found sits in a thread from October 2015. Five fetchlands from the same set, same rarity, same function in a deck. Four of them printed new all-time highs. The fifth lagged, and the only thing separating it from its four siblings was that it had been reprinted again in a supplemental product. Same demand, same game, same week. One decision by one company, and one of the five behaved differently from the other four. If you want the risk of owning a single card rendered as a picture, that is the picture.

what a box actually is

A box is a claim on the container. It resolves to cards only if someone chooses to make it, and until then it is two things bundled together.

The first is a probability distribution over contents. This part is not mysterious and it is not unpriced. Every card that could be in the box has a public price, so anyone with a spreadsheet can estimate what a box is worth if opened, and plenty of people do. That estimate is the floor under the box, loosely, and it moves with the singles.

The second thing is stranger and is the reason I own boxes at all. You are also buying an option on never having to find out. A single card is marked, every day, against what the world thinks of it today. It has already resolved and it resolves again each morning. A box has not resolved and does not have to. The distribution stays a distribution for as long as the owner declines to collapse it, and a claim that has not yet been settled behaves differently from one that has.

That difference is easy to overstate, and I am going to overstate it and then take some of it back. Overstated version: a box is diversified across a whole set, so a ban that guts one card barely dents it, while the same ban can cut a single in half. True enough. Now the correction. The box is diversified across the cards and concentrated in something the single is not concentrated in, which is the continued relevance of the entire set. A single card can outlive its set by being useful somewhere else. A box cannot. If nobody has a reason to open a set, the box holding that set is not a diversified basket of anything. It is a wrapper.

Which brings up the line I trust most in this whole business. Buffett said it about businesses, and it fits here better than it fits most places: "Price is what you pay, value is what you get." With a sealed box the price is observable to the cent and the value is not observable at all, and the peculiarity of this asset is that the value stays unobservable even after you own it.

the pack that costs more than the box

On 13 May of this year I deployed roughly 1,952 dollars across six positions in a single day, the busiest day in this book's short history. Among them: one Antiquities booster pack at 535.00, one Urza's Saga Tournament Pack at 481.48, one Revised Edition booster pack at 300.66, one Urza's Saga booster pack at 288.89, one Urza's Legacy booster pack at 147.93.

Read that list again with the earlier one beside it. Three days later I bought an entire modern Set Booster box, a whole box of packs, for 337.10. So a single sealed pack from the mid-1990s cost me more than a whole box of a set released in 2023, and a different single pack cost me most of a second one.

That price relationship is not an anomaly to be arbitraged. It is the two assets telling you what they are. The old pack is a claim on a print run that closed decades ago, whose surviving population only ever falls, and whose contents include material the publisher has promised never to reprint. The modern box is a claim on a print run that a company chose recently, in a quantity it has never disclosed, in a year when it had every commercial reason to choose a large one. One of those is a fact about the past. The other is a forecast about a corporation.

And yet the best-performing position in this book is neither. It is six sleeved set booster packs of Tales of Middle-earth, bought off a retail shelf on 6 June 2024 for 51.30, the first purchase I ever made here, now marked at 84.36 for a gain of 64.4%. Six packs from a shop. No vintage pedigree, no closed print run, no clever thesis. I have not found a satisfying story for why that is my best position and I am suspicious of the ones I have tried, which is usually the sign that a result is smaller than it looks.

a number that should worry you

Before I close, an admission that costs me nothing to make and would cost something to leave out.

Five of the vintage positions from 13 May are marked, to the decimal, at exactly +5.0%. Five different products, three different decades of printing, five different collector bases, all carrying the identical return. No market does that. That mark is a straight-line model running between observation points, not an observation, and anyone reading the portfolio page should discount tidy numbers accordingly. Marks in this book come from public retail and marketplace data, including listed prices as well as completed sales, and a listed price is an opinion held by a seller.

So which is the better asset, the single or the box? Wrong question, and fifteen years of forum argument is the evidence that it is the wrong question. A single is a verdict the market re-delivers every morning. A box is a question you have paid money to keep asking.

Letter 001 · 30 June 2026 · Miami, Florida

The machine that prints the asset

In February 2011, someone posted a question on a trading card forum asking whether it was worth buying some old school booster boxes. He had a little money, he liked the game, and he wanted to know if the boxes in the back of a shop were an investment or just cardboard he would be stuck with. The replies were split, as they always are. Some told him scarcity would take care of him. Some told him he was confusing a hobby with a portfolio.

I read that thread this month, fifteen years after it was written, and what struck me was not that the question was hard. It is that the question has not changed. I have an archive of 316 of these threads now, running from 2011 to this year, and the same argument recurs in almost every one of them, in the same words, with new set names dropped in. Nobody in 2011 knew the answer. Judging by the 2026 threads, nobody knows it now.

That should tell you something before we go a single step further. When a market has been asking itself the same question for fifteen years without converging, the question is usually posed wrong.

two flows, not one

Here is how I have come to think about it, and I want to write it down at the start because every letter after this one will lean on it.

Sealed trading card product sits between two opposing flows. Supply is created by a single publisher, at a rate it chooses, in quantities it does not disclose. Supply is destroyed by consumption, because every box that gets opened leaves the sealed float permanently and does not come back. Price is what happens in the gap between those two flows. Demand matters, of course it does, but demand is the thing everyone already watches. The mispricings live on the supply side, because one end of it is opaque and the other end is voluntary.

The destruction side has a property I have not seen in any other asset I have held. Most collectibles lose supply slowly and against their owners' wishes, through damage, loss, fire, time. Sealed product loses supply quickly and on purpose, and the purpose belongs to exactly the people whose enthusiasm holds the price up. When a set is exciting, people open it. The float contracts fastest precisely when the bid is strongest, and it stops contracting the moment interest fades.

Sit with that, because it cuts both ways and the second way is the one that costs money. A set everyone loves eats its own supply, which is the bull case. A set nobody cares about keeps its supply forever, which is not the bear case people think it is. It is worse. It means the unloved sealed product from the unloved set, the one that looks cheap and rare on a shelf, is rarely the bargain it appears to be. There is a thread in my archive from October 2025 asking whether older booster boxes are simply dead shelf product at this point. It is a good question and the answer is often yes.

Scarcity without an audience is just inventory. I would like that sentence to survive this letter, because a lot of bad purchases start with forgetting it.

the creation side, where it gets harder

Print runs are set by a company measured on quarterly revenue. That is the whole of it. Every other consideration in this market is downstream of that one fact.

The demonstrated pattern is that when demand surprises to the upside, supply follows. Final Fantasy is the case study, and the publisher's own framing is more damning than anything a critic could write. The Lord of the Rings crossover took about six months to generate 200 million dollars of revenue. Final Fantasy did the same in a single day. The print run was raised repeatedly and the market was still short.

Now, that is a company behaving rationally. I want to be careful here, because there is a strain of commentary in this market that treats the publisher as a villain for printing things, which is roughly like calling a bakery greedy for baking. It is also a company demonstrating, in public and at scale, that it will not protect the scarcity of anything it can still print. Those two statements are both true and only the second one is actionable.

the only real commitment device

Which brings me to the Reserved List, and to why the calmest part of this book sits in cards printed before I could drive.

The Reserved List is a published promise not to reprint a specific set of older cards. It has been honoured for three decades. Whatever you think of the policy, and there are good arguments that it is bad for the game, it does something that nothing else in this market does: it turns scarcity-by-restraint into scarcity-by-fact. Everything anchored to it has behaved less like a speculation and more like a store of value. That is not exciting. That is the entire attraction.

So where does that leave the fashionable end of the market, which is also, awkwardly, a large part of my book? The optimistic argument is that a licence constrains the publisher in a way its own intellectual property does not. It cannot simply reprint a Tolkien set whenever it likes, because the rights are not its own.

There is something to that. There is less to it than holders would like. Tales of Middle-earth arrived in 2023. The Hobbit arrives in 2026, from the same corner of the same universe. The licence did not prevent a second bite. It only shaped it. Anyone who bought in 2023 believing they held the only Tolkien Magic product that would ever exist has now learned what that belief was worth, and I say that as someone holding 550 dollars of 2023 Tolkien product, the best performing line in this book, who believed a softer version of the same thing.

the question I will actually ask

All of this reduces to one question, and I intend to ask it of every position in this book, including the ones I already regret.

Is the scarcity here a fact or a forecast?

Out-of-print material from the 1990s is a fact. The print run closed before most of the current audience was born, and the only open question is how many survived. A crossover set released this year is a forecast: about a publisher's restraint, about a licensor's appetite, about whether an audience assembled by a film franchise stays assembled. Both can belong in a portfolio. I hold roughly 48% in the first kind and about 12% in a single set of the second kind, so I am plainly not a purist about it.

What they must not be is confused with each other, or sized as though they carry the same risk. Most of the money lost in this market, I suspect, has been lost by people who owned a forecast and thought they owned a fact.

The man in 2011 asking about old booster boxes was closer to a good answer than he knew. He was not asking whether cardboard goes up. He was asking who decides.
