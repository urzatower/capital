Note 001 · 29 August 2026

Float is the whole thesis

Every argument for holding sealed product reduces to one observation: supply is fixed at the end of the print run and the float shrinks from there. Nothing else about the asset is unusual. It pays no coupon, it has no earnings, and the only reason a box is worth more in five years is that fewer boxes exist and someone still wants what is inside. That is a supply story, not a demand story, and it is worth being honest that the demand side does the heavy lifting on the way up.

The float mechanic is unusual in one respect. Most collectibles lose supply slowly, through loss and damage. Sealed product loses supply through consumption, and consumption is voluntary, immediate, and correlated with exactly the enthusiasm that supports the price. When a set gets popular, people open it. The float contracts fastest precisely when the bid is strongest. That is a reflexive loop and it cuts both ways: interest fades, opening stops, and the remaining float sits there permanently.

Which is why the reprint is the only risk worth watching closely. A reprint does not compete with the original print run on condition or provenance, but it competes directly on the thing people actually buy the product for, which is the cards inside. The publisher controls that supply and has every commercial reason to use it. Anyone holding sealed product is short an option they did not sell and do not get paid for. The mitigation is not clever, it is just selection: favour sets where the reprint would be legally or commercially awkward, and keep individual entries small enough that being wrong about one of them does not matter.

The book is positioned accordingly. Roughly two fifths of net asset value sits in out-of-print material from the 1990s and early 2000s, where the print run is closed and the reprint question is settled by history rather than by a licensing decision. The rest sits in crossover product, where the audience extends past the game and the licence itself constrains what the publisher can do later. Concentration is the honest weakness: the top ten positions are just under two thirds of the book, and every position is exposed to a single publisher.

One last point on marks. Every position in the book is currently above cost, and that is not a signal of skill. Most of the capital went to work within the last four months, marks are taken from public retail and marketplace data rather than from realized sales, and no position has ever been sold. Until something is sold, the entire return is an accounting statement about other people's listings.
