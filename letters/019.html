Letter 019 · 29 August 2026 · Miami, Florida

Everything I own is green

There is a particular pleasure in opening a spreadsheet where every line is green. I have forty-one positions in this book, seventy-six sealed units, five buckets, and as of this week every single one of them is marked above what I paid. The book shows 8,063.68 of invested capital carried at 8,436.55. Up 4.62%. Nothing red, nothing to explain, nothing to apologise for at the bottom of a letter.

I looked at that page for a while last week and enjoyed it, and then a small unpleasant thought arrived, which is the thought that should always arrive: what would it take for this page to look exactly like this if I were completely wrong about everything?

Not much, as it turns out. That is what this letter is about.

three reasons the page is green

The first is timing. Roughly 96% of the cost basis in this book was deployed after 1 May 2026. The first purchase was in June 2024 and the book formally began in November 2024, so on paper it has a two-year history, but the money does not. In money terms this is a three-month-old portfolio wearing a two-year-old coat. A three-month-old portfolio marked by any model with a positive drift will be green. That is not a finding. That is a definition.

The second is the marks themselves. They come from public retail and marketplace data, which includes listings as well as completed sales. A listing is an ask. An ask is somebody's opinion about what their box is worth, published by a person with an obvious interest in that opinion being high, and in thin markets the asks sit well above the trades because the sellers who would take less have already sold and left. When I mark to a mixture of asks and trades, I am marking to a number that is systematically friendly to me.

The third is the simplest and the worst. Nothing in this book has ever been sold. Not one unit, not one pack, in two years. Every gain on that page is a gain I have asserted and nobody has yet disputed with money.

the tight band

Now the part I did not spell out clearly enough in earlier letters, and should have.

Every position I have acquired since 1 August is marked between plus 1.7% and plus 2.0%. Every one. That covers a Fifth Edition starter deck bought at 141.23, a sealed Fallen Empires booster box at 534.99, an Ice Age starter deck from 1995 at 207.58, and a modern Hobbit Collector Booster Box at 795.00. Four products from four separate eras of printing, bought through two different channels, with entirely different buyers, entirely different float and entirely different reasons for anyone to want them. And they agree with each other, to within about thirty basis points.

No market does that. Arithmetic does that.

The mechanism is not hidden, I built it: the net asset value path between marks is straight-line modelled and there are no intramonth marks. Which means a recent position's reported return is largely a function of how many days ago I bought it. It is a clock, not a price. When I look at that band of 1.7 to 2.0 I am not observing four assets appreciating in unison. I am observing my own spreadsheet counting.

And it is not only August. Go back to 13 May, the busiest day this book has ever had, when roughly 1,952 dollars went out across six positions. Five of those were vintage. An Antiquities booster pack at 535.00 carried at 561.83. An Urza's Saga Tournament Pack at 481.48 carried at 505.63. A Revised booster pack at 300.66 carried at 315.74. An Urza's Saga booster pack at 288.89 carried at 303.38. An Urza's Legacy pack at 147.93 carried at 155.35. Every one of them marked at exactly plus 5.0%. Four different sets, printed years apart, with different survivorship, different collector bases and different reasons to be scarce, all appreciating by precisely the same percentage to the first decimal place.

If a strategist showed me a portfolio like that in my actual job I would ask one question and it would not be about the assets.

the position that proves it

I have written before about the Fallen Empires box, and I am going to keep writing about it until the accounting changes, because it is the cleanest test of the method I own.

Bought 8 August at 534.99. Carried at 543.92, which is to say plus 1.7%, which is to say inside the band, which is to say the model. Meanwhile recent completed sales on the same marketplace for the same product printed in the 365 to 386 range. If those completed sales are the price, I am not up 1.7% on that box. I am down somewhere between a quarter and a third of it.

Both numbers cannot be true. One of them came from what people asked. The other came from what people paid.

Here is the part I glossed over the first time. If the gap is real on that position, the gap is not a property of that position. It is a property of the method, and the method has no way of knowing which of the other forty positions it is currently wrong about. I found this one because I went looking on a box I felt uneasy about. Unease is not a screening process. It is the opposite of one: it finds exactly the errors you were already half expecting and leaves the rest sitting there in green.

Two changes are committed. Valuation moves to a sold-price basis wherever completed sales exist. And the Hobbit block, which is about 34.8% of the book, gets reviewed against post-release prices in October rather than against listings written by people who own it.

the part of the truth I do not publish

One more omission, since I am cataloguing them. The portfolio page shows the invested book. It does not show the cash. There is 2,227.02 sitting in cash, about 21% of total assets, and it has earned nothing and appreciated by nothing and appears nowhere on the page a reader sees. So the return this book advertises is the return on the part of me that was invested and happened to be right, presented without the part of me that was waiting.

Contrast all this with the three positions I actually trust. A six-pack of Lord of the Rings sleeved set boosters bought off a shelf in June 2024 at 51.30, carried at 84.36. A starter kit at 21.39, carried at 32.13. A scene box at 23.99, carried at 35.45. Up 64.4%, 50.2% and 47.8%. What makes those marks credible is not that the method changed. It is that time has had a chance to disagree with them and has not. Two years, a full release cycle, a sequel announcement, and the numbers held. A mark you have carried through events is worth more than a mark you have carried through a week.

And that is the actual lesson, which has very little to do with cardboard. The dangerous moment in any book is not the drawdown. A drawdown makes you check everything: the inputs, the sizing, the thesis, the man who sold it to you. Nobody has ever lost money because they audited too aggressively while losing. The dangerous moment is the one I am in, where the number is pleasant and the pleasantness is manufactured by an assumption nobody has stress tested, including me, especially me.

Marks put the working discipline about as briefly as it can be put: you can't predict, you can prepare. I would add a smaller and less elegant rule for personal use. When the page is green, go and find out why before someone else does.

Because I am not up 4.62%. I am up 4.62% according to me, and so far there is nobody on the other side of that sentence.
