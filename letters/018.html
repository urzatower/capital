Letter 018 · 28 August 2026 · Miami, Florida

The cash is a position

The most interesting line in a portfolio is almost always the one that is not on the page. I have believed this for years about other people's books and I have been slow to apply it to my own.

There is a thread in my archive from October 2025 in which someone lays out their Magic holdings as a financial portfolio, item by item, with what was paid and what it is supposedly worth. It is a serious piece of work and I recognised the impulse behind it immediately, because it is the same impulse that produced this fund. What I noticed, reading it, is what I notice in every accounting of this kind, including the ones I have written. It is a list of purchases. The money that was not spent does not appear anywhere, because money that was not spent does not have a picture next to it.

My own portfolio page has the same shape. It reports 41 positions, 76 sealed units, five strategy buckets, 8,063.68 dollars of invested capital marked at 8,436.55, up 4.62% and nothing ever sold. All of that is true and all of it is incomplete. The ledger behind the page has a forty-second line the website does not report: 2,227.02 dollars in cash, sitting beside a book of 8,436.55. Call it 21% of total assets.

That is a large number to leave unexplained, and an unexplained large cash balance is read by everybody, correctly, as indecision. So this letter is the explanation. I would rather defend the cash than have it look like a man who could not make up his mind.

what cash is for, and what it is not for here

In an ordinary fund, cash does two jobs at once and they get conflated. The first job is defensive plumbing. You hold cash because investors can ask for their money back, because a clearing house can ask for margin, because the fee bill arrives whether or not the year went well, because a fund that is forced to sell something in order to pay for something else has already lost the argument before it starts. That cash is not an investment decision. It is a liability sitting on the asset side of the page, waiting to be claimed by somebody.

The second job is the one everybody talks about and almost nobody is actually free to do: the cash exists to be spent at a price somebody else sets in a moment of stress.

Now look at this book. There is no outside capital, so nobody can redeem. There is no leverage, so nobody can call me. There are no fees, so there is no bill arriving in January. The product sits in my own custody, so there is no counterparty who can decide to be difficult on a bad week. Every structural reason to hold cash defensively has been removed, not by cleverness but by the accident of the book being small and self-funded.

Which means the 2,227.02 is not a buffer. It is not prudence, it is not risk management, it is not dry powder in the apologetic sense people use that phrase when they have missed a rally. It is the most purely offensive asset in the fund. It is the only line in the ledger that can be pointed at anything, at any price, on any day, without asking a single other position for permission. Everything else I own has already committed to an opinion. The cash has not, and that is the entire point of it.

I want to put that the other way round too, because it is easy to nod at and hard to hold on to. Every dollar of cash is a dollar that has declined to buy something. That is a real decision with a real cost, taken 41 times over by not becoming a forty-second position. If that decision is never exercised, it was not patience. It was avoidance wearing patience as a costume.

the rule and the money only work as a pair

In an earlier letter I committed to a hard rule: no acquisition inside the thirty days before a set's release date. That window, between the end of preview season and the day product reaches shelves, is where I have made my most expensive mistake, and I put the rule in writing precisely so that it would be embarrassing to break.

Here is the part I did not say at the time. A rule like that is worth nothing on its own.

Consider a buyer who is fully invested and holds no cash. He can announce any restriction he likes about not buying into a release, and it costs him nothing, because he could not have bought anyway. He has not written down a rule. He has written down an opinion, and opinions that cannot be tested are free. Now consider the opposite man, holding a fifth of his assets in cash with no rule at all. Every presale, every countdown, every thread telling him supply will be tight at release is aimed directly at him, and he has the means to act on all of it. He has not written down a rule either. He has written down a temptation.

The cash makes the rule expensive, which is what makes it a rule. The rule makes the cash survive contact with a release calendar, which is what makes it cash. Neither half functions alone, and I do not think I understood that when I wrote either of them down.

what it is waiting for

Waiting is only respectable if you can say what you are waiting for. Otherwise it is a mood.

What I am waiting for is a pattern I have now watched twice. Collector Booster boxes for the Spider-Man and Avatar crossovers were bid toward 1,000 dollars in presale and settled back to roughly 750 and 525 once product actually reached shelves. Not a crash, not a scandal, just the ordinary deflation of a price set by people who could not yet buy the thing, replaced by a price set by people who could. Final Fantasy went the other way and recovered strongly, on the back of a chase card that stayed genuinely scarce in sealed product. Two outcomes, and the difference between them is knowable after release and mostly unknowable before it.

The calendar is the gift here. 2026 carries seven sets, with Reality Fracture and Star Trek still to come, and the Hobbit block faces its own review against post-release prices in October. I do not know what any of those will do. I do know when they happen. An asset class that schedules its supply events in advance is handing a patient buyer a series of dated windows, and the only thing required to be there is money that has not already been spent.

the honest cost

I owe you the other side of this, because a letter that only argues for its own conclusion is advertising.

Cash underperforms in a rising book and outperforms in a falling one. That is arithmetic, not a forecast, and it does not become insight by being said confidently. The book has been rising. All 41 positions are marked above cost, the fund shows 4.62% unrealized, and every dollar parked in cash across those months earned none of it. If the next year looks like the last few months, the cash will have cost me real return and this letter will read as a rationalisation. I accept that. I would rather be caught defending an idle fifth of the book than caught explaining why I bought at a presale price with borrowed conviction.

What justifies the drag is not a market view. It is the structure of the thing. Seven scheduled supply events a year, each one a public countdown followed by a price discovered in daylight, is an unusual gift to somebody who is under no obligation to participate. Most investors pay for optionality. Here it is free, and the only price of admission is being liquid on a date somebody else chose.

The 41 positions are what I have decided. The forty-second line is what I have refused to decide yet, and on a long enough calendar that refusal is the position I expect to be proudest of.
