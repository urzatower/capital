Letter 021 · 1 September 2026 · Miami, Florida

The lottery is the product

Secret Lair is now sold in randomized booster packs. Nineteen dollars and ninety two cents each, limit twelve per person, seven cards to a pack: three commons, two uncommons, one rare or mythic, and one wildcard, across three treatments. That was posted to a card finance board on 25 August and I have not stopped thinking about it since.

You need to know what Secret Lair used to be for that sentence to land. It was the fixed-content product. You saw the list, you knew exactly what arrived in the box, and it was printed to demand inside an ordering window, so the print run was however many people clicked. No randomness, no allocation, no hunting. Whatever else you thought of it, it was the one corner of this market where the buyer knew what he was buying.

That has now been reversed. Not modified. Reversed.

what randomizing actually buys the seller

Print-to-demand has a commercial problem, and it is worth stating plainly because it explains almost everything happening this month. If you print exactly as many as are ordered, you cannot manufacture scarcity, and if there is no scarcity there is no urgency. Fixed contents make it worse: I know precisely what the box is worth to me, so I buy one or none.

Randomization and a purchase cap solve both at once. Seven cards you did not choose creates variance, and variance is what makes a person buy the second pack. A limit of twelve creates the impression of a constrained allocation whether or not one exists. Together they convert a catalogue item into a lottery ticket, and lottery tickets have a demand curve that catalogue items do not.

I have no complaint about this as commerce. It is a good idea and it will work. But letter 008 argued that print-to-demand had quietly killed scarcity-by-print-run, and I want to correct myself in public: it did not kill it, it replaced it with something more useful to the seller. Scarcity by window has become scarcity by chance. A closed print run is a fact you can verify afterwards. A one-in-something is a probability you can be sold repeatedly.

eleven in one month

The cadence deserves a number, and I want to give it honestly rather than dramatically.

My archive carries eleven separate Secret Lair or superdrop threads dated August 2026. For the six years before that, the archive carries roughly one a year. That comparison overstates the case and I am not going to pretend otherwise, because the archive was assembled by scraping a live feed in August, so recent months are captured far more densely than old ones. The thread count is partly a measure of my own collection method.

What is not an artifact is the product list. In August alone there was a Hobbit superdrop, a Masters of the Universe superdrop, one titled A Perfectly Normal Superdrop, a Reality Fracture Secret Lair bundle, a drop from a streetwear label, an Encyclopedia of Magic drop, and the randomized packs. Those are distinct products with distinct names, and you can count them without reference to how anybody scrapes anything.

So the honest version is this. I cannot prove the conversation grew elevenfold. I can prove the supply of direct-to-consumer product in a single month is now large enough that a person who follows this market closely would struggle to name all of it from memory. That is the relevant fact for anyone holding sealed inventory, and it points the same direction the issuer's accounts pointed in letter 005.

a specimen

On 14 August somebody posted a valuation of the Reality Fracture Secret Lair bundle. The reasoning ran roughly like this: the comparable bundle from the previous set was well liked, this one also contains two collector boosters, the words Secret Lair in the name imply unusual art, the set is built around marquee planeswalkers so one of them probably receives an exclusive treatment, there are ten cards so the count of each will be low, and some of the alternate art in the set is anime styled, which historically sells for more. The conclusion was that the bundle looked close to a safe purchase.

Every individual step there is reasonable. I want to be careful not to sneer, because I recognise the structure exactly: it is the reasoning I confessed to in letter 006, when I paid 839.85 for a Hobbit Collector Booster Display against 455.88 of retail cardboard and then discovered I had assembled the arguments after choosing the conclusion.

But look at what the chain is made of. Probably. Implies. Likelihood. Historically. Every link is an inference about a product whose contents have not been revealed, and the output of that chain is the word safe. A conclusion cannot be more certain than its least certain input, and this one is a stack of guesses about unrevealed cards resolving to a purchase recommendation.

and then there is the actual set

Reality Fracture releases shortly. On 28 August a post appeared suggesting its play booster supply may be tight at release, filed under distributor allocations.

Tight supply at release is the oldest bullish claim in this market and also the least verifiable one in it. Nobody outside the company knows the print run. What people observe is their own shop's allocation, which is a sample of one, and the aggregation of many such samples into a belief about scarcity is how presale prices get made. Sometimes it is true. The Spider-Man and Avatar crossovers were also said to be tight, were bid toward a thousand dollars, and settled back to roughly 750 and 525 once product actually reached shelves.

Which brings this to a decision rather than an observation, because Reality Fracture is the first real test of the rule I wrote down in letter 019: no acquisition inside the thirty days before a set's release date. There is 2,227.02 sitting in cash. The set is arriving, the supply chatter has started, the Secret Lair bundle attached to it is being valued in public by people who sound confident, and my own rule forbids me from doing anything about any of it until thirty days after release.

That is exactly what the rule is for, and it is the first time it has cost me anything, which is the only kind of test a rule ever gets. A rule you have never wanted to break is not a rule. It is a preference you have not yet been charged for.
