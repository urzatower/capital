Letter 021 · 1 September 2026 · Miami, Florida

Thirty one up, none down

On Sunday evening I refreshed the ledger behind this site and republished the portfolio page. It is a small piece of housekeeping, the sort of thing that takes four minutes and does not normally produce a thought. The marks now run to 30 August rather than 28 August. Two days of new data.

In those two days I bought nothing and sold nothing. I have bought nothing since 24 August and I have never sold anything at all. The book did not change. Net asset value went from 8,436.55 to 8,442.66.

Six dollars and eleven cents. The unrealized return on the book went from 4.62% to 4.70%, which is eight basis points of performance generated by a man sitting still.

I said in letter 020 that it was the last of the sequence, and I meant it. This is not a twenty-first instalment of the same argument. It is a note about something I noticed while doing the filing, which turned out to be the clearest evidence yet for a thing I had only asserted.

the distribution is the story

Six dollars is not interesting. How the six dollars was distributed is very interesting.

I lined up the new marks against the old ones, position by position. Of the forty I could match cleanly between the two files, thirty one were marked up. Nine did not move at all.

Not one was marked down. Zero.

Sit with that for a moment, because I think most readers will skate over it. Forty positions in a collectibles market, repriced across two days, and the number that fell was none of them. If you handed that distribution to anyone who has spent time around real price series, they would not conclude that I am a good buyer. They would conclude that whatever produced those numbers is not a market.

A price series where nothing ever falls is not a price series. It is a model with a data feed attached, and the model has a direction baked into it.

and the nine that did not move

Now the part that made me stop and write this down.

The nine positions that did not move at all are not a random nine. They are, exactly and without exception, the nine positions I acquired in August. Every single thing I bought last month is frozen where it was, sitting at the modelled two percent I complained about at length in letter 019. Everything older drifted up.

So the pattern is not merely that nothing falls. It is that recent purchases sit inert at a number the model assigned them, and older purchases accrete gains with the passage of time rather than with the arrival of information. That is not a description of a market forming an opinion about my holdings. That is a description of a straight line being drawn between a purchase price and a mark, and me reading the line back as though it were news.

I want to be fair to the method, because I chose it and I still think it is defensible. The site says plainly that the path between marks is straight-line modelled, and it has said so since the first version. Nobody is being deceived. But there is a difference between disclosing a limitation in the footer and understanding, in your stomach, what the limitation does to the number at the top of the page. I disclosed it in June. I understood it on Sunday.

the one I said was wrong

There is a detail in the frozen nine that I could not have designed better as a joke at my own expense.

One of them is the sealed Fallen Empires Booster Box I bought on 8 August for 534.99. It is still carried at 543.92, unchanged, up 1.67%. This is the position I devoted letter 012 to, under the title Listed is not sold, on the grounds that recent completed sales for the same product had printed between 365 and 386, and that carrying it near 544 was therefore an act of optimism rather than measurement.

Two days of fresh data arrived. Thirty one positions were revalued upward. The one position I have publicly declared to be wrong was not touched.

I do not know the mechanism and I am not going to invent one. Thin products with few listings can sit unchanged for long stretches, and that is the boring and probably correct explanation. But the effect is worth naming regardless of cause: my marking process is most confident exactly where I have the least confidence, and it updates most readily on the positions I never worried about.

why publish it at all

A reasonable person could ask why I would refresh a page whose central number I have just spent four paragraphs undermining.

Three reasons. The first is that the alternative is worse. If I overrode the feed and marked the book to my own judgment, the number would become an opinion about my own performance issued by the person being assessed. A flawed external input beats a flattering internal one, and it beats it even when the flaw runs in my favour, which this one does.

The second is that the correction is already scheduled rather than merely promised. The move to a sold-price basis where completed sales exist was committed to in letter 012, and the Hobbit block gets reviewed against post-release prices in October. The right response to a bad mark is to fix the policy on a date, not to argue with the feed on a Sunday.

The third is the one that actually matters. A published number you have criticised in public is a number that cannot quietly become your track record. Every one of these letters stays on this page. When the 4.70% eventually meets a buyer and turns into something smaller, the argument for why it was always going to be smaller will already be sitting three paragraphs above it, written before the fact and dated.

All 41 positions remain above cost. There is still 2,227.02 in cash and there is still nothing to show for any of it, because a gain you have not sold is a sentence you have not finished.
