Letter 015 · 21 August 2026 · Miami, Florida

Who is on the other side

Every sealed box in this book is a promise. The promise is quite specific: that inside the shrink wrap there are the correct number of packs, from the correct set, printed in the year the label says, untouched since a machine in a factory closed them. I have never verified that promise on a single unit I own. Neither has anyone else in the chain. The promise has simply been passed along, from whoever made it to whoever believed it, and I am the current holder of the belief.

So the question that ought to sit at the front of this entire enterprise is not what the print run was, or what the chase card is, or what the publisher reports next quarter. It is much older and much duller than any of that. Who made the promise, and what happens to them if it turns out to be false?

I have written fifteen letters about supply. This is the first one about the counterparty, which tells you something about my own priorities that I do not love.

the audit that destroys the asset

Start with what makes this asset class genuinely odd, because it is not the cardboard.

Every other thing I have ever held can be checked without being consumed. A bond has a trustee, a coupon record, a registrar somewhere with your name on a list. A share is an entry in a system built specifically so that ownership can be confirmed by a stranger. Even a physical bar of metal can be assayed, and afterwards it is still a bar of metal, slightly smaller and considerably better documented.

A sealed box can be verified in exactly one way. You open it. And when you open it you have destroyed the thing whose authenticity you were confirming, because the seal was the asset. Not the cards. The seal. The audit and the position are mutually exclusive, and there is no version of this where you get both.

Sit with what that implies about price formation. The value is not in the contents. The value is in an unexamined claim about the contents, and every participant in the market knows the claim will almost certainly never be examined, because examining it is the one action that guarantees the value goes away. The whole thing runs on the shared understanding that nobody will check.

It gets worse with time, and in the wrong direction. The older and rarer a sealed box becomes, the more it is worth, and the more it is worth, the more insane it would be to open it, and the less likely anybody ever is to find out. Verification risk compounds in exactly the same place where value compounds. The most expensive box in any collection is definitionally the one least likely to ever be opened, which makes it the one least likely to ever be proved real.

what the archive argues about, and what it does not

My archive runs to 316 threads across fifteen years, tagged by theme. The distribution is worth reading as a document in its own right. Reprint risk and supply shocks, 87 threads. Commander demand, 83. Distributor allocations and wholesale supply, 73. Retail dumps, 53. Flash sales and cycle bottoms, 53. Licensed intellectual property, 44. Bans and format governance, 41. Buyouts and price spikes, 33. Vintage and Reserved List scarcity, 32.

Nine themes. Not one of them is about whether the box is real.

The threads exist, they are simply scattered and unloved. In January 2015 someone posted a warning about being taken for 1,200 dollars on eBay. In August 2017 someone asked, plainly and without much hope, where a person could find sealed sellers who could be trusted. And this month, August 2026, there are warnings circulating that deals which look too good to be true are exactly that, including when they come from sources that appear reputable.

Read those three dates together. Eleven years separate the first from the last, the market grew by an order of magnitude in between, the publisher reported its first half billion dollar quarter in July, and the state of the art in counterparty diligence is still a stranger on a forum saying be careful. Nothing was built. No standard emerged. The market spent fifteen years arguing about how many boxes exist and almost none of it asking whether any given box is one of them.

five doors, and the money went through the weakest one

This book buys through five channels: eBay, Amazon, TCG Marketplace, Best Buy and Target. They are usually treated as interchangeable, as places where the price is. They are not interchangeable at all, and the difference has nothing to do with price.

At one end is the retail shelf. The first purchase this book ever made was six sleeved set booster packs from a national electronics chain in June 2024, for 51.30. The custody chain on that is publisher, distributor, retailer, me. Four steps, all of them corporate, all of them recorded, none of them anonymous. If those packs are wrong, an enormous company with lawyers is wrong, and the recourse is obvious. It is the most verifiable position in the book. It is also one of the smallest.

At the other end is the open marketplace, where the platform is a venue rather than a seller, where the counterparty may be a shop or a person or an estate, and where the object may have spent thirty years in one closet or thirty years across six of them. Here is where the money actually went. The Antiquities booster pack at 535.00, bought on eBay. The Urza's Saga Tournament Pack at 481.48, eBay. The Revised pack at 300.66, the Urza's Saga pack at 288.89, the Urza's Legacy pack at 147.93, all eBay, all on the same day in May. The Fallen Empires box at 534.99, eBay, in August.

The Vintage and Legacy bucket is about 41% of net asset value, 3,294.20 of cost across twenty-one units. It is the oldest material I own, the most valuable per unit, the part I have argued most confidently is anchored to a real and durable scarcity, and the part whose provenance is least documented and least documentable. Most of it was printed before the market existed as a market, and nobody kept the kind of records that would now settle an argument.

So the relationship between verifiability and value in this book is precisely inverted from how you would design it if you were designing it. Where the promise is easiest to check, I have the least money. Where it is impossible to check, I have the most.

what self custody does and does not solve

This book has no outside capital, no fees, no leverage and no redemptions. It holds everything in self custody, sealed, controlled by me. I have written about that as a strength, and it is one. It answers theft, loss, damage, humidity, sunlight, and the risk of sitting on somebody else's balance sheet at the wrong moment.

Every one of those is a storage risk. Not one of them is an authenticity risk. Custody answers the question of who can take the box away from me. It says nothing whatsoever about whether the box was ever what the listing claimed. I have built a careful answer to the second-order problem and left the first-order problem exactly where I found it, which is in the hands of people I have never met and cannot name.

I am not going to pretend I have a solution, because I do not. What I can do is small and mostly obvious: buy at retail where retail exists, prefer sellers with long histories and platforms with real buyer protection, keep the photographs and the records at purchase rather than at the moment of doubt, and stop treating a vintage sealed box as if it carried the same risk profile as a pack lifted off a retail shelf and carried to a till.

And then there is the thing that genuinely bothers me. There is no number for this. I can quote a cost, a mark, a bucket weight, a percentage of net asset value. There is no authenticity spread, no percentile, no probability I could honestly attach. So it appears in none of my tables, and a risk that appears in none of your tables is a risk you have quietly decided is zero.

Every other risk in this book, I can at least go and look at. This one, I would have to destroy the asset to see.
