Letter 027 · 15 September 2026 · Miami, Florida

Who pays for the box

Letter 009 asked who the marginal buyer is and then let the question go. It has been sitting in the archive for six weeks looking at me. Twenty-six letters about supply, print runs, allocation, float and the publisher's decisions, and almost nothing about the person on the other end who has to want the object enough to pay for it. That is a strange balance for a book whose entire return depends on a stranger's discretionary spending in some year I cannot name.

Supply is the easy side because it is knowable. Somebody printed a number of boxes, the number is finite, and reasoning about it feels like analysis. Demand is the hard side because it is a collection of household decisions made by people who are not thinking about me. So of course the archive is full of supply and thin on demand. The available evidence shapes the thinking, which is exactly the trap.

the buyer is a household, not a market

Every price in this book is eventually paid by somebody spending money he did not have to spend. Not a fund, not an allocation, not a mandate. A person choosing a sealed box over a weekend away, a console, a repair he has been deferring. That single fact has three consequences and I have been treating none of them seriously.

The first is that this demand is late-cycle in the household sense. Discretionary spending on collectibles is not the first thing a person cuts, but it is nowhere near the last thing he protects. A book of sealed product is therefore long consumer confidence with extra steps, and it is long it in a form that cannot be sold quickly when confidence goes.

The second is that the buyer's budget is shared. He is not choosing between two Magic boxes. He is choosing between a Magic box, a Pokemon bundle, a graded card, a video game collector's edition and a concert ticket. Which is a good moment to disclose that the fund now owns a Pokemon 30th Celebration bundle and a World of Warcraft collector's edition, at 144.97 and 190.46 dollars, together 2.7 percent of cost. Two positions do not make a diversification strategy. They make an admission: the competition for the collector's wallet is now inside the book rather than only outside it, and I would rather own a little of it and understand it than write another letter pretending the wallet is ours alone.

The third is that the buyer arrives through a channel, and the channel takes a cut. Every exit in this market crosses a marketplace that charges a fee, a shipping cost that has been rising for years, and in the case of anything valuable, a grading or authentication step that costs money and time. The marks in this book are gross of all of it. A position marked at plus 6 percent is not plus 6 percent to me.

what would actually damage this book

Not a reprint. I have written about reprints six times and they are a known hazard with a visible trigger. The thing that would damage this book is quieter: two or three years in which the marginal buyer simply does not show up, because the film has come and gone, the audience has moved to the next crossover, and the household that was going to buy a sealed Hobbit box in 2028 bought something else instead.

Nothing about that scenario produces a headline. There is no announcement, no thread, no obvious day on which it happened. It looks exactly like a market that has gone quiet, and this market goes quiet all the time. Letter 017 described the shop shelf where a price has not moved in four years and called it the absence of a trade rather than a price that held. The whole-market version of that shelf is the risk nobody writes about, including me until now.

how you would see it coming, if you could

Honestly, late and imperfectly. But there are observable things, and starting now they belong in the monthly reading: whether a set's price decline continues past the point where the product has fully arrived, whether dealer bids widen away from dealer asks, whether the gap between listed and completed prices grows, and whether new releases keep clearing at all. Those four are visible in the data the fund already collects, and none of them is a forecast. They are the market telling you how well it is absorbing what it is being sold.

The Hobbit is the live test. It released on 14 August, the play box has fallen through its preorder level and kept going, and the fund owns eight lines of it marked between minus 9 and minus 17.7 percent. If demand for a major crossover set is this soft while the product is new, that is a demand-side reading and not a supply story, and I should treat it as one.

what this changed in the book

Two things. The monthly readings now include the four demand signals above, so the gauge stops being purely about price and starts being about absorption. And the buying rule from letter 025 gains a clause: nothing new in The Hobbit until the play box stops making lower marks, because adding to a position while the demand evidence is deteriorating is the exact behaviour letter 014 described and did not stop.

Cash stays at 2,227.02 dollars, which after this letter I am going to stop describing as uninvested. It is the only position in the book with no exposure to whether a stranger feels like spending money this year.
