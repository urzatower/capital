Letter 005 · 15 July 2026 · Miami, Florida

Reading the issuer's accounts

I spent an evening last week doing something dull. I went through the theme tags on the fund's archive and counted. Eighty-seven threads tagged reprint risk and supply shocks. Seventy-three tagged distributor allocations and wholesale supply. Fifty-three tagged retail dumps. Fifty-three more on flash sales and cycle bottoms. Set aside the overlap and you are still looking at the better part of two hundred conversations, spread across fifteen years, whose entire subject is how much product exists and who is holding it back.

Then I went looking for the threads that answered the question by reading the company's accounts. I could not find one. Not a bad one, not a wrong one. I could not find a discussion in the archive that was built on the issuer's own audited numbers.

Think about how strange that is. This is a market that talks about supply constantly, to the point of tedium, with real money at stake and real anger in the posts. The supply is set by a single publicly listed company. That company files quarterly. The filings are free, they are audited, they are indexed, they are searchable, and they are the only document in this entire market that somebody has signed their name to under legal penalty. Everything else is a rumour about a pallet.

I have a theory about why. People here think of the company as the maker of a game they love or resent, which is a relationship, not an analysis. Almost nobody thinks of it as the issuer of the thing they own. But that is exactly what it is. If you hold sealed product, you hold an asset whose supply is decided by a public company with a quarterly revenue target and an investor call to get through.

the income statement is a supply schedule

Here is the sentence I want to put at the centre of this letter, and I am aware it will sound backwards the first time you read it.

Record revenue is record supply.

In 2025, Magic revenue reached 1.72 billion dollars, up roughly 59% on the prior year, carried by the Final Fantasy crossover. Every write-up I saw treated that as a demand number. It is a demand number. It is also, and more usefully to anyone holding sealed boxes, a receipt. Revenue is what the company collected for cardboard that was printed, packed, shipped and paid for. A 59% increase in what was collected is not a signal that the market is hungry. It is a measurement of how much got made.

Now let me argue with myself, because the point is softer than I just made it sound. Revenue is price times units, and the mix in this market has been shifting toward more expensive packaging for years. Collector Boosters carry a higher price than the product they replaced. So revenue can rise faster than units, and some of that 59% is people paying more for the same number of cards rather than more cards existing. That is a real objection and I do not want to wave it away.

It does not rescue the position, though. It only tells you the number overstates the unit growth by some amount you cannot measure from outside. Direction is still direction. A company that collected 1.72 billion dollars in a year printed more than a company that collected considerably less, and the sealed float that existed at the start of that year was competing with all of it by the end.

what the company says out loud

The filings are the audited part. The commentary around them is unaudited and, for my purposes, at least as informative, because it tells you what the organisation is being pointed at.

The framing I keep returning to is the company's own comparison of its two biggest crossovers. The Lord of the Rings set took about six months to generate 200 million dollars of revenue. Final Fantasy did the same in a single day. That was offered to investors as a triumph, and it was one. Read it from where I sit, holding sealed product, and it is something else. It is a public statement, by management, about which kind of release earns applause. Nobody inside that building needed a memo after that sentence was said out loud.

Then there is the calendar, which is the part I find most useful and which is not hidden anywhere. 2026 carries seven sets. Four are crossovers, three are Magic's own worlds. It is the first year in the game's history in which borrowed universes outnumber original ones. Lorwyn Eclipsed in January, Teenage Mutant Ninja Turtles in March, Secrets of Strixhaven in April, Marvel Super Heroes in June, The Hobbit in August, with Reality Fracture and Star Trek still ahead of us. That list was public well before most of those sets were released. It is not a rumour and it is not a leak. It is a supply schedule, published a year in advance, and anyone buying sealed product in this market had it available for free.

The company's head designer has called 2026 an outlier and signalled a return to three in-universe sets and three crossovers. I believe he means it. I also note that this is guidance, not a covenant. There is a difference between a company saying what it intends and a company binding itself, and the whole of my confidence in the older end of my book rests on that difference. The Reserved List is a promise not to earn revenue from certain cards, kept for three decades. It appears nowhere on the income statement, because a promise not to sell something never does. That absence is the most valuable line item the company has.

what I will do next Tuesday

The second quarter numbers arrive on Tuesday. I am not going to trade them, because I do not trade, and because a quarterly print is far too fast a clock for an asset I intend to hold for years. I am going to read them the way I would read the accounts of a company whose paper I own, which is what they are.

What I want from the document is not the growth rate. It is whether the growth is coming from more product or dearer product, what the margin does while that happens, and what the company says about the second half of a year that still has two sets in it. If a company is spending more to sell more, its incentive to keep selling more does not weaken. It hardens.

I say all this as someone with a foot on both sides. On 13 May I deployed roughly 1,952 dollars across six positions in material printed in the 1990s, where the print run closed before most of the current audience was born and no filing on earth can change the number of boxes that exist. In June I put 502.33 and 534.95 into two Marvel Super Heroes Collector Booster cases, product from a set printed this year by a company that has told its investors, in plain language, that it intends to print more. Both purchases were deliberate. Only one of them is a fact rather than a forecast, and I would rather write that down now than discover it later.

This market spends its evenings guessing at the print run. The company publishes it four times a year and calls it revenue.
