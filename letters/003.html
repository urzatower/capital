Letter 003 · 7 July 2026 · Miami, Florida

The box you cannot open

In August 2013, someone posted a question that has stayed with me longer than almost anything else in this archive. He owned several Modern Masters booster boxes. He wanted to open one. He could not decide, so he asked a forum full of strangers to tell him what to do.

Sit with the shape of that for a second. The boxes were his. He had paid for them and nobody was stopping him. He was not short of information either, since he knew what the box cost and roughly what was inside. He was asking for permission, and people ask for permission when the decision in front of them is not really about money.

I think he had noticed something real without having the vocabulary for it. He had worked out that the box in his hand was not a box of cards. It was a box of cards plus something else, and the something else would not survive the act of finding out what the cards were. Opening it would exercise the option and destroy the asset in the same motion, with one thumbnail, in under a minute, permanently.

Financial markets do not offer many instruments like that. An option on a stock can be exercised and the stock is still there afterwards. A bond redeems and the issuer carries on. Here the act of collection annihilates the thing collected. He was right to hesitate. He was also, in a way he could not have known, standing at one of the tiny hinges on which the entire supply side of this market turns.

float destruction, seen from the inside

I wrote in the first of these letters that sealed product loses supply quickly and on purpose, and that the purpose belongs to the very people whose enthusiasm holds the price up. That is a view from above. The 2013 thread is the same fact seen from inside a living room.

The number of sealed boxes of any set in the world is not a figure the publisher sets. The publisher sets one term of it. The other term is the running total of thousands of private decisions like that man's, each made for reasons that have nothing to do with anybody's portfolio. Someone opens a box because a friend is coming over. Someone opens one because they got a raise, or lost a job, or moved apartments and got tired of carrying it. The float is the residue of all of that.

Which produces the reflexive property that I find the most interesting thing about this asset class and the hardest to trade around. The float contracts fastest when the bid is strongest. Excitement about a set is simultaneously the reason people want sealed boxes and the reason those boxes stop existing. Demand eats its own supply.

Now let me argue against myself, because the loop does not run away the way that description suggests. Every dollar the sealed box rises raises the cost of opening it. At some point the box is worth conspicuously more than the cards likely to come out of it, and at that point the rational owner stops being a consumer and becomes a holder. Destruction slows exactly as the price justifies it. The float, in other words, is anchored to the gap between what a box costs and what it is expected to yield when opened, and that gap is self-correcting.

Then run it the other way, which is the direction that costs money. There is a thread from March 2017 observing that after a set gets hit by a Masters reprint, it becomes one of the lowest value sets to open. When the expected contents collapse, nobody opens the boxes. So supply stops shrinking at precisely the moment demand for the contents falls away. The float freezes at whatever the print run happened to be, and it stays frozen forever. Scarcity without an audience, again. This market has a talent for offering you the wrong scarcity at the wrong time.

the rule, and its problem

This book holds 31 positions and 39 sealed units. Not one of them has ever been opened. Nothing has ever been sold either, but the sealed rule is the stricter of the two, because a sale is reversible in the sense that the same product can be bought back, and an opening is not reversible in any sense at all.

The rule is not sentiment about wrappers. It exists for two dull reasons. First, an opened box stops being comparable to itself: the position turns into a pile of singles that must be marked individually, and a book that mixes the two is a book whose returns cannot be audited by anybody, including me. Second, opening converts a distribution into a realisation, and a realisation is a far easier thing to be publicly wrong about. So the rule is 100% sealed, permanently, and it is written down where I cannot quietly amend it.

Here is the problem with all of that, and I would rather write it myself than have a reader find it. A rule that has never been tested is not yet a rule. It is a preference with good grammar.

Roughly 93% of this book's cost basis was deployed after 1 May 2026. The rule has therefore been in force, under anything resembling real temptation, for a period measured in weeks. I hold two Marvel Super Heroes collector units bought in early June, at 502.33 and 534.95, and I have not opened either. I would like to report that as discipline. Honestly, it is not, because I have not once wanted to. Refusing something you do not want is not restraint, it is arithmetic.

The genuine test is coming and I can see the date on it. The Hobbit arrives in August, and it has a candidate chase card, roughly 500 copies in existence, turning up in fewer than one in a hundred Collector Boosters. That is the configuration that breaks rules. Not a card worth a fortune, a card worth a fortune at odds bad enough that a reasonable person can talk himself into one more box. Whatever collector product I hold going into that release will be sitting there through the exact weeks when opening it is most exciting, most social, and most defensible after the fact.

Howard Marks has a line I keep coming back to: "You can't predict. You can prepare." I cannot predict how I will feel in August. Writing the rule down in July, in public, with a date on it, is the only preparation available.

what the option is really worth

I have been calling the sealed box an option, and I should be honest about where that language stops working, because borrowed vocabulary flatters an argument in ways the argument has not earned.

It resembles an option in the ways that matter most. It has an underlying whose price is publicly observable, since every card that could be inside is quoted somewhere. It has no expiry. It has a carry cost, in storage, in the shelf space, in the capital not doing something else. And it has an asymmetry that anyone who has held one recognises, which is that the good outcome is much better than the bad outcome is bad.

Where it stops resembling an option is on the other side of the ledger. In a real options market you know how many contracts are outstanding. Here nobody does. The print run is undisclosed and the destruction rate is unmeasurable, so the one quantity that would let you price the thing properly is the one quantity nobody has. Every valuation of sealed product, mine included, is an estimate of contents dressed up as an estimate of scarcity.

Which is why I keep returning to a stranger's post from thirteen years ago. He was not asking whether to open a box. He was asking someone to tell him what he owned. Nobody could, and nobody can, and that is the position.

Answers are the cheap part of this market. The expensive part is the wrapper that stops you from having one.
