Letter 014 · 15 August 2026 · Miami, Florida

The second bite

On 13 August I paid 795 dollars for a Hobbit Collector Booster Box. It was an ordinary purchase, made the ordinary way, and I felt the ordinary small pleasure of it. Then I went back to the book to record the entry, and the spreadsheet did the thing spreadsheets do, which is to put two facts next to each other that you had been keeping in separate rooms.

The best performing bucket I own is Lord of the Rings: Tales of Middle-earth. It cost me 550.29, it is up strongly, by a wider margin than anything else in the book, and it has been the one line I have quietly enjoyed looking at since I started. Directly underneath it, larger by a factor of four, sits The Hobbit. And I had just added to The Hobbit.

I want to describe that feeling accurately, because I do not think I have had it before in any other market. It is not the feeling of a hedge. A hedge is something you buy on purpose, knowing what it offsets. This was the feeling of realising that the thing I had just bought because I liked it is also, structurally, the thing most likely to take away the number I liked most. I had spent 795 dollars to compete with myself, and I had done it cheerfully.

what the licence was supposed to do

Go back to the argument I made in the first of these letters, because this is where it gets tested.

The publisher can print its own worlds as often as it likes. Nothing restrains it except commercial judgement, and commercial judgement, on the evidence, points at printing more. The optimistic case for the licensed sets was always that borrowed intellectual property behaves differently. The publisher cannot simply decide to run a Tolkien set again next quarter, because the rights are not its own and there is a second party who must agree. A licence, on this reading, is a supply constraint that the in-universe sets do not have.

I believed a softer version of that. I want to be honest about the softness, because the soft version is how most people hold a wrong idea. I did not think a second Tolkien product was impossible. I thought it was distant, and I thought distance was enough.

Here is the flaw, and it is not subtle once you say it out loud. A licence governs who may print. It says almost nothing about how much, or how often, or how soon. Tales of Middle-earth arrived in 2023. The Hobbit arrived in 2026. Same author, same world, adjacent stories, three years apart. Whatever the contract says, it plainly did not say once.

And look at the year around it. 2026 carries seven sets, four of them crossovers and three of them in-universe, the first year in the game's history that borrowed worlds outnumber original ones. The company's own head of design has called 2026 an outlier and signalled a return to three and three. Perhaps. But a market that assumed licensed product was rationed by contract has just watched the ration double, and the correction to the schedule came after the fact, not before it.

what a second bite does to the first one

The cleanest evidence I have on this question is not from crossovers at all. It is from a pair of threads in my archive about a group of lands.

In August 2014, someone tracked dealer prices for the Onslaught fetchlands before and after a reprint was announced. Those cards had not been printed since 2002. They were reprinted in September 2014, and the archive has the prices on both sides of the announcement, which is rarer than it sounds, because most of this market's memory is anecdote.

The better thread is from October 2015. By then every Khans fetchland had reached an all-time high, with one exception: the single land that had been printed a second time in a supplemental product. Five cards. Same set, same rarity, same function, same demand, same formats. Four of them made new highs. The fifth, the one that got a second bite, lagged.

That is about as close to a controlled experiment as this market will ever hand you. Every variable held constant except one, and the one that moved was supply, and the effect was visible in the price of a specific card in a specific month. I do not want to overfit it. A sealed box is not a single card, a new set from the same licence is not a reprint of the same product, and the collector demand for a 2023 Tolkien box does not have to behave like the tournament demand for a land. The direction, though, is the direction. When the market learns that a thing it thought was finished is not finished, it re-rates the thing that already exists, and it does not re-rate it upward.

the thread from May

In May of this year, before any of the Hobbit product existed as an object, my archive picks up a thread about what the Hobbit reveals mean for the Lord of the Rings cards people already own. Note the timing. The set was still being revealed. Nothing had shipped, nothing had been opened, no price had printed. What the thread contains is a group of holders doing the arithmetic in public, in real time, on the value of a position they bought in 2023 under an assumption that was in the process of being cancelled.

This is a sentiment record, not a price record, and I would not lean on it for a mark. But reveal season is exactly when belief gets repriced, and belief is most of what a collector market is made of. Everything after that is confirmation, arriving slowly, at retail.

Howard Marks has a line about this that I think about often: experience is what you get when you didn't get what you wanted. Those 2023 holders are getting experience. So am I. I own 550.29 of cost in the product they are worried about.

on both sides of my own trade

So here is the awkward accounting, stated plainly, because if I do not state it someone else will.

My best performing bucket is 2023 Tolkien, ahead of everything else I own, and it is the product being diluted. My largest thematic exposure is The Hobbit, 2,200.83 of cost across seven units, about 29.7% of the book, and it is the diluting agent. The book is on both sides of its own trade. That sounds balanced. It is not balanced, and the imbalance is the whole point: I have roughly four times as much money in the new thing as in the old one. If a second bite transfers value from 2023 to 2026, I collect it. If it destroys value in both, which is the outcome the 2015 thread does not rule out, I pay for it four times over on one side and once on the other.

And I am not paying retail for the privilege. I have already put in print that I bought a Hobbit Collector Booster Display on 18 July for 839.85, when the publisher priced a Collector Booster at 37.99, which puts twelve of them at 455.88 at the counter. Roughly eighty percent over the cost of the cardboard, bought between the end of preview season and release, which the record says is the worst window there is. The box I bought on 13 August cost less than the July one. Less is not the same as cheap.

What would make it cheap is if this set behaves like Final Fantasy rather than like the two crossovers before it. Collector boxes for the Spider-Man and Avatar sets were bid toward 1,000 in presale and settled back to roughly 750 and 525 once product actually reached shelves. Final Fantasy went the other way and recovered strongly, on the back of a chase card that stayed genuinely scarce inside sealed product. The Hobbit has a candidate: the Gleaming Gold Smaug, around 500 copies, appearing in under one percent of Collector Boosters. That is scarce enough to matter. Whether it is wanted enough to matter is the open question, and I have roughly 30% of the book expressed as an answer to it.

Two rules are already committed. The Hobbit block gets reviewed against post-release prices in October rather than against the listings I am currently marking to. And I do not buy inside the thirty days before a release date again, which is a rule written in the specific shape of the July mistake.

I did not hedge my Tolkien exposure this month. I concentrated it, and told myself it was a different set.

A licence has never stopped anyone from printing. It only decides whose name goes on the next box.
