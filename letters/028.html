Letter 028 · 30 September 2026 · Miami, Florida

The box has no coupon

On 17 September the Federal Reserve raised its target range for the first time in this cycle, to 4.00 percent at the top. That is the smaller half of the story. The larger half is what the long end did next. The thirty year Treasury, which paid 4.73 percent a year ago and 5.29 percent on the day of the meeting, pays 5.59 percent today. The ten year sits at 5.26 percent, its inflation-protected version at 2.91 percent, and the gap between two and ten years has widened to 41 basis points with the long end leading. A tightening cycle where the long bond sells off harder than the front end is not a market congratulating the central bank on its discipline.

Two days from now, on 2 October, Reality Fracture goes on sale. This fund owns a collector booster box of it bought on 7 September for 641.95 dollars, marked at 509.40 today, and a play booster box bought the following day for 149.79, marked at 166.03. One is down a fifth before the set exists. The other is up a tenth. Neither has been opened, nothing has been sold, and the difference between them is the entire subject of this letter.

The sealed market is up 27.0 percent over twelve months and 11.4 percent over three. The book is up 33.4 percent on what it cost. And 25 of 51 positions are below their purchase price, which is the same uncomfortable arithmetic as last month: the headline is one Lord of the Rings box and the rest is a long tail doing very little.

the number nobody in this hobby discusses

Read any thread about sealed product and you will find print runs, allocations, reprint risk and chase cards. I have an archive of 316 of them and the tags prove it. What you will not find, in any of them, is a discount rate.

That is a strange omission, because a sealed box has no coupon. It pays nothing while you hold it. It does not rent, it does not yield, and holding it costs money in storage and in the insurance you probably have not bought. Its entire value is a payment somebody might make for it at an unknown date, which makes its present value a distant number divided by one plus a rate, raised to a power. When that rate moves, the value moves, and nobody has to post a thread about it for the arithmetic to happen.

The rate has moved a great deal. A long-dated real yield near 2.9 percent is not a historical oddity, but it is a different world from the one in which most of the sealed market's current holders formed their expectations. For a decade the alternative to holding cardboard paid approximately nothing. Today the alternative pays 5.59 percent a year, in cash, guaranteed by the Treasury, with no shipping cost, no marketplace fee, no grading step and no risk that a publisher reprints your position.

One of the cards in this set, Perfected Theory, describes Jace seeing himself reflected in a Multiverse complete in every detail, and deciding that all he had to do was make it real. Every discounted valuation ever written has that quality. The model is complete and the rate is the part that decides whether any of it survives contact with a buyer.

Put the two together and you get a hurdle rather than an opinion. To beat a thirty year bond over five years, a box bought today must appreciate by roughly a third in nominal terms, and that is before the ten to fifteen percent that a marketplace sale takes in fees and shipping. Before this year, the hurdle was closer to a tenth. The set did not change. The alternative did.

second-level thinking about a market that has not noticed

Here is what makes the period interesting rather than merely expensive. The sealed market is up 27 percent over the last twelve months, which is the same twelve months in which the long bond lost a great deal of value. The discount rate rose and the asset that should be most sensitive to it rose too.

First-level thinking says the market is ignoring rates and will eventually be taught a lesson. Second-level thinking asks who the buyer actually is, and whether that buyer discounts anything at all. I think the honest answer is that the marginal buyer of a sealed box is not performing this calculation. He is a collector with a hobby budget, and his decision is between a box and a weekend away, not between a box and a long bond. His hurdle is not 5.59 percent. It is whatever else he wanted that month.

If that is right, two things follow, and they point in opposite directions. The comforting one is that this market has a demand base that does not reprice with the curve, which is why it has held up. The uncomfortable one is that the same base is tied to discretionary income, and a tightening cycle that eventually bites employment reaches a hobby budget sooner than it reaches a pension fund's allocation. Rates do not damage this market through the discount rate. They damage it through the buyer's payslip.

I would rather own an asset whose risk arrives slowly through the labour market than one that reprices the day a yield moves. But I should be honest that the second risk, the one I am supposedly exposed to, is not the one that will actually show up.

what reality fracture actually is

Before the arithmetic, the thing itself, because I have been writing about this set for three letters without once describing it.

Reality Fracture is a Magic set of 283 cards that releases on 2 October, with a companion Commander release beside it. The story is the darkest version of a Jace Beleren story that Wizards has published. Jace has rewritten the Multiverse into a construction called the Echoverse, built by excising the things he judged intolerable: the Eldritch Horrors, the Phyrexians and Nicol Bolas. The result does not hold. It is unstable, paradoxical, and leaking back into the real Multiverse.

To do it he split himself. The fragment in charge is called The Theorist, and he erases minds and puppets people to protect the project, while another fragment, the part that stayed honest, narrates the official guide and regrets what he has become. The setting is Hexhaven Academy, a magic school inside a tower that has warped into impossible architecture, with five disciplines taught by elder sphinxes: divination, reality-bending mathematics, emotional manipulation through song, the animation of puppetbeasts, and healing by invasive plant grafting.

The flavour text on the cards is unusually direct about the theme. One card carries The Theorist saying to a victim, "I only wish you could see the necessity of this. Your counterparts all have." Another describes him cutting away every part of his own mind that objected to the plan.

The card list says the same thing mechanically. There are two Ajanis, Resolute and Unrelenting. Two Garruks, Curse Breaker and Veiled Butcher. Two Lilianas, the Faultless and the Repentant. Two Chandras, Torch of Defiance and Chill of Compliance. The marquee mechanic makes Jace tokens. A set about a man who split himself is sold as a set of doubled cards, which is a tidy piece of design and, as it happens, a tidy piece of product engineering too.

The pairs are not duplicates. Each one prints a character twice: the person their history made, and the person Jace's edit made of them. Karn appears as the Argent Defender, carrying "a life without rest, a soul heavy with regret," and again as the Gilded Guardian of "a world without blemishes, a soul without anguish." Mabel is a bitter recluse whose village was destroyed, and a valley hero who learned that mother and protector were not far apart. Proft solves intricate crimes on one card and commits them on the other. Hapatra mourns her god on one and usurps him on the other.

I did not expect a card game to hand me the better metaphor for a portfolio, but there it is. Every position in this book has an echoed pair: the version that exists, marked daily at a price I can check, and the version in which I waited a week, or bought the other box, or kept the cash. The second one is always cheaper, always better behaved, and entirely imaginary. The honest way to run a book is to mark the first and refuse to argue with the second.

the set as a product, which is the part that prices my boxes

Here is where a collector's set and an investor's set stop being the same object.

The main set contains 26 mythic rares. Our own catalogue, which counts every distinct printing rather than every distinct card, holds 78 mythic entries for this set. Three quarters of the mythic layer is not card, it is treatment: borderless echoed pairs, a Braintwister series, sculptor's stronghold frames, portal view lands, tower basics, a ten-card panorama by a single artist, Japan Showcase foils.

Above all of that sits one card. A serialised Bloodline Recollector, 500 copies, collector boosters only, currently quoted between roughly 2,300 and 3,500 dollars. Below it, Shattered Mirror variants at about 6,000 copies per rare and 3,000 per mythic.

There is a card in this set called Murmuring Volume whose flavour text reads that its pages surrender no words until the book has first read its prospective reader. That is roughly the relationship between a collector booster and the person buying it.

So a collector booster box at 27 dollars a pack is, in plain terms, a wrapper around a 500-ticket lottery and a thin layer of scarce finishes. That is not a criticism of the design. It is a description of what the price is made of, and it explains why the two boxes in this book behave so differently. The collector box is priced on a distribution whose tail nobody has sampled yet. The play box is priced on cards people intend to use, with a community thread in late August warning that play booster supply may be tight at release.

Which is the whole letter, arriving from a different direction. The collector box is the longest duration asset in this book: no cash flow, a payoff concentrated in a tail, and a value that depends entirely on what somebody will pay later. At a 2.9 percent real discount rate, that is exactly the kind of claim that gets marked down first, and it is the one down 20.6 percent before the set exists. The play box, the boring one with a supply constraint and a user base, is up 10.8 percent.

reality fracture, and the preorder window again

Both boxes were bought inside the preorder window, a day apart, before the product existed, at prices the market set on anticipation. Twenty-three days later one is down a fifth and the other is up a tenth.

Letter 024 described the shape of a preorder curve across two sets: prices drift down as release approaches and the product becomes real, with the collector version holding longer because fewer people have one. This set is tracing that curve in the opposite order, which tells me the shape is not a law. It is a tendency with enough variance that buying inside the window is a coin flip dressed as an entry.

The rule from letter 025 would have stopped the collector box. It was a purchase inside a preorder window, above the last observed trade, in a bucket already heavy. The rule arrived three weeks too late and cost about 130 dollars, which is a cheap price for finding out the rule was right.

Reality Fracture is also a reminder of what this hobby is now. A Magic set arriving between a randomised Secret Lair experiment, a Pokemon anniversary product and a Warcraft collector's edition, all four of which are in this book. The product calendar has become continuous. When supply arrives every few weeks, scarcity stops being a property of the market and becomes a property of a specific print run, which is a much narrower thing to underwrite, and a much shorter-lived one.

what this changed in the book

One addition to the buying rule, and it is the discount rate finally appearing in a letter about cardboard. From today, no new position unless the case for it clears the thirty year Treasury plus the cost of exiting, over a realistic holding period. At 5.59 percent and roughly twelve percent of exit friction, that is a demanding test, and it should be. Most of what I have bought this year would not pass it.

The Theorist, in the voice the set gives him, tells a victim: "I only wish you could see the necessity of this. Your counterparts all have." It is the sound of a man explaining why the rules do not apply to his particular case. Every holder who has ever averaged down into a falling mark has heard that voice, usually in his own accent.

Nothing was sold, and the Reality Fracture boxes stay. The cash balance of 2,227.02 dollars also stays, and I will note that it is now the only position in this book that earns anything at all while it waits. That has not been true for most of the time I have been doing this, and it is the quietest argument against buying the next box.
