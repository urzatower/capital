Letter 025 · 15 September 2026 · Miami, Florida

Where we stand

Twenty-four letters in, I have written a great deal about what things mean and almost nothing about where we are. Those are different questions. The first one is interesting and can be argued forever. The second one is boring, answerable, and the only one that changes what a person should do this month. Howard Marks has spent thirty years making that distinction the centre of his writing, and the line I keep coming back to is the one about taking the market's temperature: you cannot predict, but you can prepare, and preparing starts with knowing whether you are standing in cheap or expensive territory.

So this letter begins something. From here, every letter carries the same six readings in the same order, taken from the fund's own marks and its own index. Not forecasts. Readings. A year from now there will be twelve of these in the archive and the sequence of them will say more than any single one does, which is the entire point. Here is the first.

Now the reading that matters, and it is not the big number. The book is up 231.5 percent since inception against 43.6 percent for the sealed market, and 18 of 51 positions are above cost while 24 sit below it. Read those two facts next to each other. Nearly half the book is losing money and the headline is a triple. That is not a contradiction and it is not an accident. It is a description of what actually happened: one position, a Lord of the Rings Special Edition collector box bought in September 2025 for 2,591.93 dollars and marked at 6,826.40 today, is carrying the fund.

Strip it out and this is a book that has roughly kept pace with the market it operates in, with more losers than winners and a middle half of positions sitting between minus 9.5 and plus 15.9 percent. That is the honest description of the enterprise. One good decision, a long tail of ordinary ones, and a market that has been rising underneath all of it for two years.

second-level thinking about a green page

First-level thinking looks at plus 231.5 percent and concludes the strategy works. Second-level thinking asks what would have to be true for that number to exist even if the strategy did not work, and in this case the answer is embarrassingly simple: buy one thing early in a set that later got a film-driven bid, and mark it daily against a feed that has repriced it upward four times this year. I did not underwrite that box as the position that would define the fund. It became that by outcome, not by design.

Which means the correct question for the next twelve months is not whether I can repeat it. It is whether the rest of the book, the part that is down, is down because I paid too much or because the market has not got there yet. Those look identical on a screen and they are not the same thing at all. I own eight lines of Hobbit product, five of them play booster boxes bought between May and September, and every one is marked below cost, between minus 9 and minus 17.7 percent. The buying happened across four months and three channels, so this is not one bad click. It is a view that the market has so far declined to share.

A price that disagrees with you for a fortnight is noise. A price that has disagreed with you across five separate entries and four months is information, and the honest handling of it is to say so in public rather than to wait for the mark to come back and then claim patience.

the temperature, in plain words

The sealed market is up 25.4 percent over twelve months and 10.7 percent over three. Product that arrives on shelves keeps repricing downward after release, while product people are still waiting for holds its preorder level. Dealer asks sit well above last trades on the thinner items. Presale packs that had no traded price at all three weeks ago now have one, and it came in above what I paid.

None of that is a cheap market. It is a market where the thing that is working is the thing that has already worked, which is the ordinary late-cycle condition in any asset that a wider audience has recently discovered. I am not calling a top, because calling tops is the activity Marks spent a career warning people about. I am saying that the readings above are what a full market looks like from the inside, and that the correct response to a full market is not to sell everything. It is to stop paying up.

what this changed in the book

Nothing was sold, because nothing has ever been sold here. What changed is the buying rule. Until the next reading, no new entry in a bucket that is already above 15 percent of marked value, no purchase of product still inside its preorder window at a price above the last observed trade, and the cash balance of 2,227.02 dollars stays where it is unless something goes on sale rather than on release. Three of those constraints would have stopped at least one purchase I made this quarter.

And a change to how these letters work. They move to monthly, with a scoring letter each quarter in which the claims made here get checked against what happened, and every letter from this one on ends with this section, so the record shows not only what I thought but what it cost me to think it.
