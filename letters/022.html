Letter 022 · 4 September 2026 · Miami, Florida

What the dragon was worth

A thread posted on 25 August passes along a fact that stopped me for most of an evening. In the summer of 1994, an Unlimited Shivan Dragon was worth more than an Unlimited Black Lotus.

Read that again if you own anything in this market. The single most famous object in the hobby, the card that now anchors the entire high end and gets treated as the closest thing this asset class has to a gold bar, was at one point worth less than a big red dragon. Not because anybody was stupid. Because in 1994 the dragon was the best thing you could actually cast, and the Lotus was an odd little accelerant whose consequences nobody had worked out yet.

Eleven days before that thread, on 14 August, a different board was running the joke that the Power Nine would be too weak for today's Standard and belongs in a beginner product. The two posts are separated by thirty two years and they are about exactly the same thing from opposite ends.

two different reasons to want an object

Anything in this market can be wanted for what it does or for what it is. Those are separate demands with separate buyers and they do not move together.

In 1994 the market priced what things did. That was rational, because the game was three years old and there was no such thing as nostalgia for it yet. Today, for those same cards, the market prices what they are. The playability of the Power Nine in current formats has fallen to approximately nothing, which is the literal content of the August joke, and their prices are at the top of the market anyway. The demand did not decline. It migrated.

That migration is the thing I had not properly named in twenty one letters. Letter 001 was about print runs and letter 007 was about reprints, and both treat supply as the variable. This is a demand mechanism, it runs one way only, and it has a clock on it.

Call it obsolescence. New cards are printed that do the old job better, the old card stops being played, and every dollar of its price that rested on playability quietly leaves. Nothing is reprinted. No supply is added. The card simply stops being useful, and if it is not yet old enough for anybody to feel sentimental about it, there is nothing underneath.

the trough

Here is the part that matters for a portfolio, because the migration is not smooth.

Value leaves on the playability side long before it arrives on the artifact side. In between there is a stretch where a product is too new to be nostalgic and too old to be played, and both of its demand sources are weak at once. That is the trough, and it is where most of this market's money goes to die.

Letter 018 asked whether older booster boxes are just dead shelf product. I think the honest answer is that a lot of what looks dead is simply in the trough, and the useful question is whether it will come out. Junk wax, from letter 004, is the case that never did: the product entered the trough on schedule and then, when nostalgia finally arrived on time in the 2010s, there was so much of it left that being wanted did not help. Sentiment showed up and found the warehouse still full.

So the trough is survivable, but only if enough of the supply was consumed on the way in. Which is the float argument from letter 003 arriving from a completely different direction, and I find that reassuring rather than repetitive. Two independent lines of reasoning landing on the same variable is usually a sign the variable is real.

my book is barbelled and I did not do it on purpose

I went back through the ledger with this in mind and found something I had never looked at directly.

The vintage sleeve is nineteen positions and 3,294.20 of cost. Its newest holdings are a Mirrodin tournament pack, a Legions theme deck and a Darksteel theme deck, all early 2000s. After that the book contains nothing whatsoever until Tales of Middle-earth in 2023. There is a two decade hole in the middle of this portfolio.

Under the argument above, that hole is exactly where the trough is, and the barbell is close to the right shape. The old end has completed its migration: an Ice Age starter deck from 1995 at 207.58, or a Fifth Edition starter deck at 141.23, contains essentially nothing that anybody plays, which means obsolescence has already taken everything it is going to take. There is no playability left to lose. Whatever those are worth, they are worth it as objects.

I want to be straight about the provenance of that shape. I did not design it. I bought old things because they were old and I bought new things because I was excited, and the gap between them is not a strategy, it is the absence of one. Having noticed it, I would rather keep it than fill it, but nobody should mistake a lucky shape for a plan.

the half with the clock on it

Which leaves the other end, and it is the larger problem, because the Hobbit block is roughly 35% of the book at cost and it has not begun the migration at all.

Whatever those boxes are worth today rests substantially on chase cards being wanted, and wanted mostly means played or chased by people who follow what is played. That is the depreciating component. Nothing protects a 2026 chase card from a 2029 card that does the same job better, and on the evidence of August the publisher is not slowing down the thing that makes 2029 cards better than 2026 cards.

The bull case is not that this will not happen. It will. The bull case is that the object survives the loss and comes out the far side wanted for what it is, which requires a Tolkien audience that outlives the format legality of the cards inside the box. That is a real possibility and it is also a thirty year bet being financed by someone who has held the position for seven weeks.

The 2,227.02 in cash exists partly for this, incidentally. Things in the trough are cheap for reasons that are true, which is what makes the trough dangerous and also what makes it the only place a patient buyer has an advantage.

The dragon was the better card. The Lotus was the better object. It took this market about thirty years to tell those two things apart, and I am carrying a third of my book as though I can do it in eighteen months.
