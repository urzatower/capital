Letter 023 · 6 September 2026 · Miami, Florida

Twelve commons

On Wednesday the publisher sold a product I did not buy, through a channel I do not use, containing cards I do not own. By Monday it will have changed which cards are legal in a tournament format. That sequence is the whole letter.

The Zeta Set went on sale on 2 September through the Secret Lair store. It is a collaboration with the art collective MSCHF, done in the visual language of the original 1993 playtest cards, and it is the first time a full set has been released through that channel rather than through distribution. The card database lists 121 distinct cards across 363 printings, every one of them nonfoil, every one of them full art, every one of them credited to the same artist. The contents are reprints of ordinary things. Lightning Bolt. Birds of Paradise. Sol Ring. Chrome Mox, which is a mythic here and trades around 167 dollars in its Double Masters printing. A Sensei's Divining Top at roughly 42, a Land Tax at 17, an Imperial Recruiter at 13.

It sold out in about thirty minutes. That is not my estimate, it is the title of a thread posted the same afternoon. Presale listings were on the secondary market before the queue had finished emptying.

the sale did not work

The publisher then did something I have not seen it do about a Secret Lair. It published a statement describing its own sale as a disaster.

The queue failed. Buyers could not tell whether their orders had gone through. Some received duplicate confirmations, some received none while apparently having been charged. The statement says the team could not yet identify whether the cause was an internal systems problem or external tampering, asks anyone unsure of their order to contact support inside 24 hours, and promises to say more when the investigation is finished.

I want to sit with that for a paragraph rather than enjoy it. This is the entity whose supply decisions determine the value of every object in my book, telling the public it does not know what happened inside its own storefront during its own sale. Letter 005 spent two thousand words reading the issuer's accounts as though the issuer were a well understood machine with a legible policy. It is a company. Companies have Wednesdays like this one.

The commercial consequence of the failure is that nobody knows how many exist. A thread on 3 September asked, seriously, whether Zeta is the smallest print run set since Alpha, and worked through an arithmetic that lands somewhere near six million cards without any way to check it. No print run was disclosed. None will be. A second thread the same day made the sharper point: this is a collaboration, and the collaborator is a brand whose entire commercial model is scarcity, so the usual expectation that the publisher will reprint into demand may not survive contact with a partner who would rather it did not.

So the float on this product is genuinely unknown, permanently, by design. That is the first time in the eleven weeks I have been writing these that I have been able to say that about anything, and I notice that the market treated it as a reason to buy rather than as a reason to be careful.

and then the rarity symbols did something

Here is the part that actually matters, and it has nothing to do with whether the packs go up.

Pauper is the format in which only cards printed at common are legal. It is a real competitive format with a real card pool, and that card pool is defined by a symbol printed in the middle of the card frame. Zeta contains 52 commons. Twelve of them are cards that had never appeared at common before: Dispatch, Return to Dust, Serra Angel, Narcomoeba, An Offer You Can't Refuse, Cut Down, Sengir Vampire, Price of Progress, Vandalblast, Gigantosaurus, Baleful Strix and Soul-Guide Lantern.

On Monday 7 September those twelve become legal in tabletop Pauper, and on 23 September in the online version. The format panel had previously decided otherwise and reversed itself, on the stated grounds that rarity is the governing standard across all products, that future Secret Lair releases will be vetted before release rather than after, and that the banned list is the correct instrument for a card that turns out to be too strong. It then used that instrument immediately, banning Narcomoeba and Price of Progress on arrival, and put Baleful Strix, Vandalblast, Dispatch and Cut Down on watch for review on 12 October and again in December.

Baleful Strix was a three dollar rare on Thursday. By Friday there was a thread on the spillover, and by Friday there was also a thread titled to the effect that somebody had bought a great many copies of Strix, Vandalblast and Dispatch. Price of Progress, an Eternal Masters uncommon at around eight dollars, is now a common that is banned in the only format its new rarity would have admitted it to.

what I actually learned

Twenty two letters of this have treated supply as the variable and rarity as a fact about a card. Print run, reprint velocity, float destruction, the marginal buyer. All of it assumes that what a card is stays fixed while how many exist moves around.

This week the publisher moved the other one. Serra Angel did not become more common in the ordinary sense, there are not meaningfully more Serra Angels in the world tonight than there were on Tuesday. What changed is the category. An art collective's set assigned twelve cards a symbol, and the symbol reached through into a tournament format and rewrote its legal card pool, and the panel that governs the format found out along with everybody else and had to ban two cards in the same announcement that admitted them.

That is a supply shock with no units in it. I have no model for it. Every framework in these letters prices quantity, and quantity is not what moved.

The honest version of the lesson is narrower than it feels. It is not that the publisher is reckless. The panel's reasoning is defensible and the pre-vetting commitment is the right fix. It is that the boutique channel now carries decisions with the same reach as the main product line, at a higher frequency, with less disclosure and, on the evidence of Wednesday, less operational control. Letter 010 counted nine years of reprints arriving in three. This is the same acceleration showing up in a place where I was not counting.

the book

None of this touches the marks. The fund owns no singles and has never bought a Secret Lair. The book stands tonight at 7,953.60 against 8,063.68 of invested capital, unrealized -1.37%, with 17 of 41 positions carrying an observed price and the other 24 held at cost because the feed does not cover them. The vintage sleeve is down 1.03%, the Marvel sleeve down 5.91%, the Hobbit and Tolkien sleeves flat because nothing in them is priced. Cash is 2,227.02.

I am not going to pretend a week of reading forum threads about a product I do not own has changed the portfolio. It has changed one line of the risk section, which is the only claim I can make with a straight face. Reprint risk in this book has always been tracked per set, meaning per announced product, on the assumption that a set is a thing you can see coming. Zeta was announced, sold and sold out inside a day, and its most consequential effect was on a format nobody thought it touched.

The thing to hold onto is the direction of the surprise. Everything I own is a bet that the number of copies of something is fixed and known and will not be added to. That bet is unaffected. But it sits inside a larger assumption, which is that the categories the market prices in are stable, and that one is now demonstrably not. I do not know how to hedge it and I am not going to invent a hedge to sound thorough.

Wednesday's sale is worth remembering for a second reason, which is less analytical and more practical. When the checkout failed, thousands of people did not know for hours whether they owned something. That is what buying from this publisher looked like on the day the product was most wanted. Every box in the vintage sleeve of this book was, at some point, sold by a system like that one, to somebody who then had to keep it sealed for thirty years for it to be worth what I paid.

Twelve commons. No units, no print run, no way to price it, and the format changes on Monday.
