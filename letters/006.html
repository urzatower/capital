Letter 006 · 19 July 2026 · Miami, Florida

I bought the top and I knew it

Yesterday afternoon, a Saturday, I clicked buy on a Hobbit Collector Booster Display at 839.85 dollars. It is now the largest position in this book by more than three hundred dollars, ahead of a sealed Antiquities booster pack that I had thought of as the anchor of the whole enterprise.

What I felt afterwards was not excitement, and it was not buyer's remorse either, which would at least have been honest. It was recognition. The page had not finished reloading and I already knew what I had done. That is the part worth writing down, because a mistake you make in ignorance is a tuition payment and a mistake you make with the arithmetic on the screen in front of you is something else entirely.

So let me put the arithmetic on the screen for you too.

The publisher priced the Collector Booster for this set at 37.99. There are twelve of them in a box. Twelve times 37.99 is 455.88. That is what the cardboard costs at the counter. I paid 839.85. I paid roughly eighty percent over retail for a product that has not been released, which means the eighty percent is not compensation for scarcity that has been demonstrated. It is a fee for conviction about scarcity that has been asserted.

And I paid it in the window between the end of preview season and the release date, which is historically the worst window in this market to be a buyer. It is the point of maximum imagination and minimum information. Every card has been revealed, so the excitement is fully priced. No product has shipped, so nobody yet knows how much of it there will be. If you were designing a moment to separate an enthusiast from his money, you would design that one.

what I actually bought

I have gone back through my own reasoning from Friday and Saturday, and I want to be precise about what I find, because vagueness here would be a kindness to myself that I have not earned.

Every input I used was true. The licence constrains the publisher in a way its own worlds do not. Tolkien product has held up better in this book than anything else of comparable vintage. Collector Boosters are the only sleeve that carries the highest-end treatments. There is a candidate chase card. All true, all defensible, all things I would say again in public.

And every one of them was selected after the fact. I did not assemble those inputs and arrive at Tolkien. I arrived at Tolkien and then assembled the inputs. This was a Tolkien position dressed as an analysis, and the tell is that I can name, right now, the counter-evidence I had in my possession and did not weigh.

Let me steelman myself for a paragraph, because I do not want this letter to be theatre either, and there is a real argument on the other side. Retail price is not a clearing price. A box at 37.99 a pack assumes you can get twelve packs at 37.99 a pack, which for a Collector Booster case is rarely true for anyone who is not standing in the right shop on the right morning. Allocation is real. The premium over retail in this product category is not a pure sentiment tax, it is partly the price of not having to hunt.

I believe that argument. I also notice that it is an argument about paying some premium and I used it to justify paying eighty percent. That is how these things work. The good reason is genuine and it is doing the work of covering an amount it was never sized to cover. If the honest allocation premium is meaningful and the honest sentiment premium is the rest, then I have paid a defensible price for one part of the position and an indefensible one for the other, and I do not get to average the two and call the whole thing reasonable.

the counter-evidence I owned and ignored

Collector Booster boxes for the Spider-Man and Avatar crossovers were bid toward 1,000 dollars in presale. Once product actually reached shelves they settled back to roughly 750 and roughly 525. Two crossovers, both enormously anticipated, both bought hard in exactly the window I bought in, both marked down substantially the moment supply became a fact rather than a question.

Final Fantasy went the other way and recovered strongly, on the back of a chase card that stayed genuinely scarce inside sealed product. That is the case I was implicitly betting on. One of three. I did not put it to myself as one of three. I put it to myself as "the good outcome is available", which is not a probability, it is a mood.

There is a thread in the archive from August 2025 noting that the Avatar set was not yet out and was already showing discounts. That was written eleven months before I clicked buy. It cost me nothing to read and I had read it. There is another from September 2025 asking, with some bewilderment, who is actually buying Collector Booster boxes, and one from June 2021 asking whether they are worth anything to the people who play the game. Those threads describe a product whose bid is narrow and whose holding base is a thin one. A February 2023 thread asks how many people are trapped holding sealed collector product. I own the archive. I am the one who assembled it. And I bought into the most crowded window of the most crowded product category in it.

the open question, honestly stated

The Hobbit has a candidate chase card, a Gleaming Gold Smaug, roughly 500 copies in existence, appearing in under one percent of Collector Boosters. On its face that is the Final Fantasy setup: a card scarce enough that opening product to chase it is a losing proposition for most people, which supports the sealed price rather than draining it.

Is 500 copies scarce enough to hold up a box price eighty percent over retail? I do not know. I want to write that plainly rather than dressing it in a range. I do not know, and the answer will not be visible until product has been on shelves long enough for the opening rate to settle, which means October at the earliest.

"I do not know" is a perfectly respectable input. It is not an acceptable input to the largest position in a book. That is the actual error here, and it is a sizing error rather than a selection error. If the Hobbit display were a third of the size it is, this letter would be a footnote instead of a confession.

the rule that comes out of it

Two things get written down as policy, effective from today rather than from whenever it becomes convenient.

First, no acquisition inside the thirty days before a set's release date. Not a preference, a rule. I do not trust myself in that window and I have now got the receipt to prove it.

Second, the entire Hobbit block gets reviewed in October against post-release prices, and the marks move to a sold-price basis wherever completed sales exist. If the honest number is lower, the honest number goes in the letter. Nothing in this book has ever been sold and I am not proposing to sell anything now. But a mark that flatters me is worse than useless, because it is the mechanism by which a bad position becomes a permanent one.

Howard Marks has a line I keep coming back to: experience is what you got when you didn't get what you wanted. I have not lost money on this position. It is too early to know anything of the sort. What I have is the experience, delivered in advance and at full price, and it turns out you can buy that at retail plus eighty percent.

I did not buy a supply-constrained asset yesterday. I bought a Tolkien book with a spreadsheet stapled to the front, and I wrote the spreadsheet myself.
