Letter 026 · 15 September 2026 · Miami, Florida

Marking the letters to market

A view costs nothing until somebody writes it down with a date on it. I have written down twenty-four of them since June, and until this letter not one of them has been checked. That is the part of this exercise most people skip, because it is the only part that can make you look bad, and it is the part Howard Marks does that almost nobody imitates: he goes back to the memo, quotes himself, and says whether the market agreed.

So here is the first scoring letter. Four claims, chosen because they were specific enough to be wrong, checked against the fund's own marks on 15 September 2026. From here this happens every quarter.

letter 006: I bought the top and I knew it

In July I paid 839.85 dollars for a Hobbit Collector Booster Display and wrote that I had bought the top with the arithmetic in front of me. The position is marked at 792.02 today, down 5.7 percent.

Score it correct, and then notice how little that is worth. I was right about the direction and I bought it anyway, which means the letter documented a thesis and a failure to act on it in the same paragraph. A view that does not change the position is a diary entry. The useful part arrived only this month, in the buying rule in letter 025, which would have blocked the purchase outright.

letter 012: listed is not sold

In August I paid 534.99 dollars for a Fallen Empires Booster Box, then checked completed sales afterwards and found recent ones at 365 to 386. The letter was about buying first and verifying second. The box is marked at 568.92 today, up 6.3 percent on what I paid.

This is the uncomfortable one. The process was wrong and the outcome was good, and if I let the outcome settle the argument I will make the same mistake again with less hesitation. What actually happened is that the market for vintage sealed moved up between the purchase and now, which was not something I knew or claimed. The lesson from letter 012 survives the profit: the check takes one click, and a check performed after the purchase is a formality.

Note also what the same bucket did elsewhere. A Fallen Empires booster pack bought in May at 19.25 is marked at 11.66, down 39.4 percent, and three earlier packs bought in November 2025 are down 8.8 percent. Same set, same shelf, opposite results, which is what dispersion looks like at the position level rather than in a summary line.

letter 019: everything I own is green

On 29 August every one of 41 positions was marked above cost and the book was up 4.62 percent. I wrote that the page was green for reasons that had nothing to do with skill, mostly timing, and asked what it would take for it to look that way if I were wrong about everything.

Two and a half weeks later, 24 of 51 positions are below cost. The claim was right and the speed of the turn was faster than I expected. Nothing about the world changed in those weeks. The book got bigger, the new entries were made at release prices rather than after them, and the mark did what marks do.

letter 023: twelve commons, and what randomisation does

On 6 September I wrote about the Zeta Set, sold randomised through a channel that used to sell fixed content. On 2 September the fund had bought four packs on the secondary market, at 87.28, 89.88, 117.10 and 119.13 dollars, from four sellers, all of them presale with no traded price in existence.

Those packs now have an observed price: 135.71 dollars. The four entries are up between 13.9 and 55.5 percent, and the spread between the best and worst entry in the same product on the same day is 41.6 percentage points. I want to be precise about what that does and does not prove. It does not prove the thesis about randomisation was right; the product has not been released. It proves that buying the same item from four sellers on one afternoon produced four materially different outcomes, which is an argument for patience in a presale rather than an argument for the thesis.

what the scoring says about the writing

Three of four claims were right and the book is 34 percent above cost, and I do not think those two sentences are related. The claims that scored well were descriptions of mechanics: how a mark behaves, what a listing is worth, why a green page is fragile. The parts of the letters that have aged worst are the parts where I described a feeling about a set and then bought it.

Which suggests the letters should be narrower and the positions fewer. The archive is good at identifying how this market works. It has no evidence yet that it can pick which box goes up.

what this changed in the book

One rule, from letter 012's scoring: no purchase of anything above 300 dollars without a completed sales check pasted into the ledger note first, which makes the check a precondition rather than a formality. It is recorded in the ledger from today.

Nothing was sold. The Hobbit lines stay, marked where they are, and the next scoring letter in December will say whether that was patience or stubbornness.
