Letter 002 · 3 July 2026 · Miami, Florida

Singles versus the box

A sealed booster box does not weigh much. A few dozen packs of cardboard, a printed sleeve, a strip of shrink wrap that has gone slightly cloudy if the box is old enough to have sat under a shop light. You can hold one in one hand. You can read every word printed on the outside of it, the set name, the pack count, the legal type running along the bottom edge. And the single thing you actually want to know is the one thing the box will not tell you.

I did this in June with the Tales of Middle-earth Set Booster box I bought in May for 337.10. I picked it up, turned it over twice, and caught myself doing arithmetic about what was inside. Then I put it back down, which is the rule in this book, and the arithmetic stayed unfinished. That unfinished arithmetic is, I have come to think, the entire product. Not a side effect of the product. The product.

Because here is the strange part of holding a sealed box. You are not holding cards. You are holding a sealed statement about cards, and the statement is probabilistic. Somewhere in there are commons that nobody has wanted since the day they were printed. Somewhere in there, possibly, is the card that pays for the box. Both of those things are true at once, and they stay true for exactly as long as you leave the wrapper alone.

The oldest fight in this market is about this and nothing else. There is a thread in my archive from November 2013 arguing singles against sealed, and another from May 2017 doing it all over again, and a second May 2017 thread that runs the most useful version of the test: it sets the cost of a booster box beside the cost of simply buying the individual cards you wanted out of it, and asks which is the better use of the money. Both sides show up. Both sides are confident. Neither wins, then or in the nine years since, and I think the reason they never win is that they are not arguing about which asset is better. They are arguing as though they own the same asset.

They do not. A single card and a sealed box are two different instruments that happen to be made of the same material, in roughly the way a bond and the company that issued it are made of the same business.

what a single actually is

A single card is a claim on demand for one card. That is the whole of it. Its simplicity is its virtue and also its exposure, because there is nothing standing between the holder and the two things that can happen to a card: people can stop wanting it, or more of it can appear.

My archive tags 87 of its 316 threads under reprint risk and supply shocks, and 83 under Commander demand. Those are the two largest theme tags by a distance, and they are not two topics. They are the numerator and the denominator of every single card's price. Demand from a format, divided by however many copies the publisher has decided exist.

The cleanest illustration I have found sits in a thread from October 2015. Five fetchlands from the same set, same rarity, same function in a deck. Four of them printed new all-time highs. The fifth lagged, and the only thing separating it from its four siblings was that it had been reprinted again in a supplemental product. Same demand, same game, same week. One decision by one company, and one of the five behaved differently from the other four. If you want the risk of owning a single card rendered as a picture, that is the picture.

what a box actually is

A box is a claim on the container. It resolves to cards only if someone chooses to make it, and until then it is two things bundled together.

The first is a probability distribution over contents. This part is not mysterious and it is not unpriced. Every card that could be in the box has a public price, so anyone with a spreadsheet can estimate what a box is worth if opened, and plenty of people do. That estimate is the floor under the box, loosely, and it moves with the singles.

The second thing is stranger and is the reason I own boxes at all. You are also buying an option on never having to find out. A single card is marked, every day, against what the world thinks of it today. It has already resolved and it resolves again each morning. A box has not resolved and does not have to. The distribution stays a distribution for as long as the owner declines to collapse it, and a claim that has not yet been settled behaves differently from one that has.

That difference is easy to overstate, and I am going to overstate it and then take some of it back. Overstated version: a box is diversified across a whole set, so a ban that guts one card barely dents it, while the same ban can cut a single in half. True enough. Now the correction. The box is diversified across the cards and concentrated in something the single is not concentrated in, which is the continued relevance of the entire set. A single card can outlive its set by being useful somewhere else. A box cannot. If nobody has a reason to open a set, the box holding that set is not a diversified basket of anything. It is a wrapper.

Which brings up the line I trust most in this whole business. Buffett said it about businesses, and it fits here better than it fits most places: "Price is what you pay, value is what you get." With a sealed box the price is observable to the cent and the value is not observable at all, and the peculiarity of this asset is that the value stays unobservable even after you own it.

the pack that costs more than the box

On 13 May of this year I deployed roughly 1,952 dollars across six positions in a single day, the busiest day in this book's short history. Among them: one Antiquities booster pack at 535.00, one Urza's Saga Tournament Pack at 481.48, one Revised Edition booster pack at 300.66, one Urza's Saga booster pack at 288.89, one Urza's Legacy booster pack at 147.93.

Read that list again with the earlier one beside it. Three days later I bought an entire modern Set Booster box, a whole box of packs, for 337.10. So a single sealed pack from the mid-1990s cost me more than a whole box of a set released in 2023, and a different single pack cost me most of a second one.

That price relationship is not an anomaly to be arbitraged. It is the two assets telling you what they are. The old pack is a claim on a print run that closed decades ago, whose surviving population only ever falls, and whose contents include material the publisher has promised never to reprint. The modern box is a claim on a print run that a company chose recently, in a quantity it has never disclosed, in a year when it had every commercial reason to choose a large one. One of those is a fact about the past. The other is a forecast about a corporation.

And yet the best-performing position in this book is neither. It is six sleeved set booster packs of Tales of Middle-earth, bought off a retail shelf on 6 June 2024 for 51.30, the first purchase I ever made here, now marked at 84.36 for a gain of 64.4%. Six packs from a shop. No vintage pedigree, no closed print run, no clever thesis. I have not found a satisfying story for why that is my best position and I am suspicious of the ones I have tried, which is usually the sign that a result is smaller than it looks.

a number that should worry you

Before I close, an admission that costs me nothing to make and would cost something to leave out.

Five of the vintage positions from 13 May are marked, to the decimal, at exactly +5.0%. Five different products, three different decades of printing, five different collector bases, all carrying the identical return. No market does that. That mark is a straight-line model running between observation points, not an observation, and anyone reading the portfolio page should discount tidy numbers accordingly. Marks in this book come from public retail and marketplace data, including listed prices as well as completed sales, and a listed price is an opinion held by a seller.

So which is the better asset, the single or the box? Wrong question, and fifteen years of forum argument is the evidence that it is the wrong question. A single is a verdict the market re-delivers every morning. A box is a question you have paid money to keep asking.
