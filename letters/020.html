Letter 020 · 30 August 2026 · Miami, Florida

What I would have to believe

A view and a position are not the same animal, and confusing them is the cheapest mistake in this business.

A view costs nothing. It can be held loosely, revised in private, softened over dinner, quietly abandoned when the facts turn. Nobody audits a view. A position is a view with money and a date stapled to it, and it has a property the view does not: it can be wrong in a way that takes something from you. That asymmetry is the whole reason to write these letters down. Written views become slightly more like positions, because they can be checked later by somebody who remembers.

This is the twentieth and last letter of the sequence, and I am not going to spend it summarising the other nineteen. A summary is a comfortable thing to write and a useless thing to read. What I want to do instead is harder and, I think, the only exercise worth doing at the end of a series like this: state the beliefs this book is actually long, one at a time, with the money attached, and then say what would have to happen for me to admit each one was wrong.

Keynes is supposed to have said, "When the facts change, I change my mind." The line is quoted approvingly by people who never specify, in advance, which facts. That is the part that matters. Deciding what would falsify you afterwards is not falsification, it is editing.

belief one: that old cardboard stays scarce and stays wanted

Roughly 41% of this book sits in vintage and legacy sealed product, most of it acquired on a single day. On 13 May 2026 I put about 1,952 dollars to work across six positions. The largest five: Antiquities at 535.00, an Urza's Saga Tournament Pack at 481.48, Revised at 300.66, Urza's Saga at 288.89, Urza's Legacy at 147.93. It was the busiest day in the fund's history and it was, in effect, one decision written six ways.

People describe that as a bet on scarcity. It is not. It is two bets wearing one coat.

The first is that the supply stays closed. That one I am comfortable with, and it is the closest thing to a fact this market offers. The print runs ended three decades ago and the Reserved List is a published promise not to reprint, honoured since before most of the current audience was born. Whatever you think of the policy, it converts scarcity by restraint into scarcity by fact, and there is nothing else in this asset class that does that.

The second bet is that somebody still wants the stuff, and nothing protects that leg at all. My archive is fairly brutal on the point. A thread from October 2025 asks whether older booster boxes are simply dead shelf product now, and it is not a stupid question. There is a thread from March 2017 observing that after a reprint, a set becomes one of the least valuable things you can open. Scarcity without an audience is inventory, and inventory that nobody is asking about does not announce its own decline. It just stops printing sales.

So what would make me fold? Not a bad month, and not a soft listing. Listings on thin vintage product are decoration. What would do it is completed sales: if, once this book marks on a sold-price basis, the vintage bucket prints realised transactions below cost across two consecutive review cycles while the listed prices stay high, then the bid has gone and only the asking price is still standing there. Wide and widening spreads between what is asked and what is paid are how this asset class dies, quietly, without a single dramatic day.

belief two: that a licence is worth more than the cardboard inside it

The Hobbit is 2,853.54 dollars of cost, 37 units, about 34.8% of net asset value and the largest thematic exposure in the fund. That is not a tilt. That is a statement.

The statement is that a licensed crossover set holds a durable premium over the cost of the product it is printed on. Here is that belief with the arithmetic exposed. Wizards priced the Collector Booster at 37.99, which puts a twelve-pack box at 455.88 at retail. On 18 July 2026 I paid 839.85 for a Collector Booster Display, which is roughly eighty percent over the cost of the cardboard inside it, and I paid it between the end of preview season and the release date, which the record says is the worst window in the calendar to buy anything.

I have written about that purchase before and I am not going to soften it now. What I will do is say what would settle it. The candidate support is a chase card, the Gleaming Gold Smaug, at roughly 500 copies and appearing in under 1% of Collector Boosters. That is the Final Fantasy shape, where a genuinely scarce card in sealed product held the whole set up. The alternative shape is Spider-Man and Avatar, both bid toward 1,000 in presale and both settling back to roughly 750 and 525 once boxes were on shelves.

The Hobbit block gets reviewed in October against post-release prices, and I put that date in print before I knew what it would say, which was the point. If the post-release path tracks the Spider-Man and Avatar shape rather than the Final Fantasy one, then the premium I paid was for an expectation and not for a card, and the correct response is to write it down, stop adding to the block entirely, and say plainly in October that a third of the book was sized on a forecast that did not arrive. Not to average down. Averaging down into a supply event is how a position becomes an identity.

belief three: that the marks mean anything

Every number I have quoted you rests on a belief so quiet it barely feels like one: that public marks approximate what could actually be realised.

This one has already been falsified once, in print, at my expense. On 8 August 2026 I bought a sealed Fallen Empires booster box for 534.99. The book carries it at 543.92. Recent completed sales for the same product printed in the 365 to 386 range. Listed prices and sold prices are different numbers, and on thin vintage sealed the gap can be a third of the value. There is no interpretation of that which flatters me.

There is a second, subtler version of the same problem sitting in the current sheet. Every position acquired since 1 August 2026 is marked between 1.7% and 2.0%. Read that again. It is not a market observation, it is an artifact of a straight-line model filling in the space between marks, and if you saw that band of returns in anybody else's book you would ask what the pricing source was. The honest description is that the most recently bought slice of this portfolio is carried at a number the model produced, not at a number anybody paid.

The fold condition here is not conditional, it is a commitment already made: valuation moves to a sold-price basis wherever completed sales exist. What I have not said before is what I expect that to do. I expect it to take the reported return down, possibly through cost. If it does, that number gets published exactly as it comes out, in the same place and the same size as the 4.62% I have been reporting all year. A fund that only publishes the marking method that flatters it does not have a marking method.

belief four: that waiting pays

The fourth belief is 2,227.02 dollars in cash, about 21% of total assets, earning nothing while the book it sits beside is marked up.

I argued two days ago that this is the most offensive asset in the fund rather than a defensive one, and I stand by the argument. But it is a belief like the others and it needs a way to be wrong. Cash held with a plan is patience. Cash held without one is a man who has run out of ideas and would prefer that to look deliberate. The two are indistinguishable from the outside and, for longer than I would like to admit, from the inside.

So here is the test I will accept. If I buy anything inside the thirty days before a release date, the rule was never a rule and the cash was never patience, it was appetite waiting for an excuse. And the reverse test, which is the one nobody sets themselves: if the dated windows come and go through the rest of 2026, Reality Fracture and Star Trek and the October Hobbit review, and this balance is still sitting here untouched at the end of it, then I am not waiting for prices. I am just afraid, and afraid is not a strategy with a positive expected return.

what none of this has earned

Now the part that undercuts everything above, which is why it goes last rather than being buried in the middle where nobody finishes.

Roughly 96% of this book's cost basis was deployed after 1 May 2026. Four months. Nothing has ever been sold, not one unit, so not a single mark in this fund has been tested against a buyer. All 41 positions are green. Every bucket is positive. The oldest holding, six sleeved packs bought off a shelf for 51.30 on 6 June 2024, is up 64.4% and remains the best performer in the book, which tells you something honest about how much of this outcome was analysis and how much was a man wandering through a store.

A book where everything is green and nothing has been sold has earned exactly no right to confidence. The unrealized 4.62% is not a track record. It is a screenshot. Track records are made of exits, and this fund has never had one. When somebody eventually sends me a price that is below my mark and I have to decide in an afternoon whether my thesis or the buyer is wrong, that will be the first real data point in twenty letters of writing.

Which leaves one thing worth saying to whoever reads this in ten years, possibly me. You will not remember which of these four beliefs turned out to be right. You will remember whether I told you in advance how I would know.
